What is NWR Stream Manager?

NWR Stream Manager is an all-new solution to streaming your local NOAA Weather Radio stations to the internet, so anyone can listen to them online. It uses an inexpensive USB radio receiver called an RTL-SDR, plugged into a computer that stays on, such as a Raspberry Pi or a small home server.

One RTL-SDR hears the entire NOAA Weather Radio band at once. That means a single dongle can stream every weather radio station you can receive, all at the same time, without retuning anything.

You control everything from a web page that you open in any browser on your computer, phone or tablet. From there you can:

  • Stream stations to online services such as GWES Weather Radio, WeatherUSA and NOAA Weather Radio Org, or to your own Icecast server.
  • Play a station through a sound card, for example to feed Emergency Alert System (EAS) equipment.
  • Record and save EAS alerts as they are broadcast.
  • Listen to your streams, or tune around the weather radio band, right in your browser.
  • Get notifications on your phone when something goes belly up.
  • Share an RTL-SDR with another NWR Stream Manager on your network.

NWR Stream Manager runs on its own in the background. Once your streams are set up, they keep running without you. When it's installed to run as a service, it also starts again on its own whenever the computer restarts.

Why NWR Stream Manager was made

For a long time, most weather radio streams were run with a physical weather radio connected to a computer. That works, but it gets costly, since each station needs its own radio, and it isn't very efficient.

People who did stream with SDR dongles usually put together a chain of separate programs to do it: an SDR program such as SDR++ or SDR#, a virtual audio cable on Windows to pass the sound along, and BUTT (Broadcast Using This Tool) to send it to the streaming service. That chain is more fragile than a stack of dominoes. These are all desktop programs that can crash or be closed by accident, and if any one of them stops, down goes your stream, potentially multiple of your streams, depending on how many streams you're running. Windows also isn't the most dependable choice for something that has to run unattended day and night.

I created NWR Stream Manager to streamline and standardize streaming NOAA Weather Radio. Everything happens in one program that is built to run unattended, and it's all set up from one web page.

What is an SDR?

SDR stands for software-defined radio. A regular radio has parts inside that are built to pick up one kind of signal, and you turn a dial to choose a station. A software-defined radio does most of that work in software instead. The hardware simply listens to a range of radio frequencies and hands everything it hears to the computer, and from there it's the computer's problem. That's both good and bad; I know.

The SDR used with NWR Stream Manager is called an RTL-SDR. It is a small USB stick, often called a dongle, a bit larger than a thumb drive, with a connector for an antenna. They are inexpensive and easy to find.

Unlike most conventional radios, which can only pick up one station at a time, an SDR can pick up multiple stations at a time if they're near enough in frequency. As such, NWR Stream Manager can receive all 7 NWR channels at the same time, allowing users to stream multiple stations with a single SDR dongle.

If you have only ever streamed with a physical weather radio, the main difference is this: instead of connecting a radio's headphone jack to a computer, you plug the RTL-SDR into the computer and connect your antenna to the RTL-SDR. NWR Stream Manager takes care of the rest.

What you need

  • An RTL-SDR dongle. One dongle can serve every station you stream.
  • An antenna that picks up the NOAA Weather Radio band, around 162 MHz. A good antenna placed high up, or outdoors, makes a bigger difference to reception than anything else.
  • A computer that stays on, running Linux, such as a Raspberry Pi or an old PC. This is the machine NWR Stream Manager runs on.
  • An internet connection, if you stream to online services.
  • A browser on any computer, phone or tablet on the same network, to set things up.

Installation instructions are in the NWR Stream Manager project page. Once it is installed and running, open its web address in your browser. When NWR Stream Manager starts, it shows the addresses it can be reached at, which usually end in :8080.

First run and creating your account

The first time you open NWR Stream Manager, you'll see a welcome page asking you to create an account. This account keeps other people on your network from messing with your streams.

  1. On the welcome page, select Next.
  2. Choose a username. It can use letters, numbers, dashes and underscores.
  3. Choose a password of at least 8 characters, and type it again to confirm it.
  4. Select Next, then Finish.

This first account is the owner account. It can do everything, including creating accounts for other people. See Accounts for more information on this.

Create your account right away. Until an account exists, anyone on your network who opens NWR Stream Manager sees the setup page. This means someone else in your house could create the owner account themselves, which wouldn't be good, would it? Open up your browser and complete the setup as soon as NWR Stream Manager is running for the first time.

Signing in

After setup, your browser asks for your username and password whenever you open NWR Stream Manager. This is your browser's own sign-in box, not part of the page. Your browser remembers that you're signed in for a while, so you won't be nagged to sign in every time.

The dashboard

After signing in, you'll see the dashboard. It gives you a quick overview of how your RTL-SDR and your streams are doing, so you can tell at a glance whether anything needs attention.

Configured SDR
Which RTL-SDR is in use and its main settings. If the RTL-SDR throws a hissy fit and decides to freeze up or randomly disconnect, a warning appears here stating the SDR requires attention. The Configure RTL-SDR link takes you to its settings, where you can reset it.
Configured streams
How many streams you have, and a short explanation of any stream that needs attention, for example because of bad reception or a streaming service it can't connect to.
Recently issued EAS alerts
The latest EAS alerts recorded by your streams, if you have EAS recording turned on.
Storage
How much disk space is left, and how much you've gobbled up with saved EAS alert recordings and I/Q recordings. A warning appears if space is running low.

The bar across the top of every page takes you to the main areas: Dashboard, Configure RTL-SDR, Manage Streams, EAS alerts (once alerts have been recorded), Help, and More, which holds everything else.

Configuring the RTL-SDR

Plug your RTL-SDR into the computer running NWR Stream Manager and connect your antenna to it. Then open Configure RTL-SDR from the bar at the top.

Under SDR source, choose RTL-SDR connected to this machine. Then pick your dongle from Active SDR. Dongles are listed by serial number. If yours isn't listed, check that it's plugged in and select Rescan.

Changes on this page take effect right away. You don't have to remember to click the save button.

Gain

Gain controls how strongly the RTL-SDR amplifies what it receives. Think of it like the volume on the antenna side, before any listening happens.

  • Keep gain between 30 and 35 dB for the best results in most places.
  • Gain that's too high overloads the dongle with other nearby signals, which causes interference and can make reception worse, not better.
  • Gain that's too low will weaken your signal and leave weak stations buried in static.

Leave Automatic gain control turned off. It tends to raise the gain too high, which, as mentioned above, can cause issues.

PPM correction

Some dongles, especially older ones, don't tune exactly to the frequency they're told to. They can end up a considerable amount off frequency, and if that isn't already bad enough, some dongles drift as they warm up. PPM correction compensates for this. You shouldn't have to play with it, but if you do, it's there. If you hear loud distortion when listening to your streams, that's probably frequency drift, and you can try tweaking the PPM until it sounds good as a troubleshooting step.

Bias tee

The bias tee sends a small amount of power up the antenna cable. It's only for equipment that needs that power to work, such as some antenna amplifiers (often called LNAs) or active antennas.

Most people will never need to turn this on. Only enable the bias tee if a piece of hardware connected to your antenna cable actually requires DC power. If you have hardware that needs it, you'll know, but for the vast majority of people, keep this setting switched off.

Alias filter strength

This controls how well NWR Stream Manager keeps signals from outside a station's channel from leaking in. Leave it at 100%. Lowering it saves some processing power on weak computers, at the cost of letting some interference through. If you slide this all the way down to 0, you'll probably hear a high-pitched whine if you're trying to listen to a weaker weather radio station and have a strong station nearby on a different frequency.

Notifications for the RTL-SDR

Once you've set up notifications, a Notify when the RTL-SDR requires attention option appears on this page. Turn it on to get a notification if the RTL-SDR disconnects, can't be opened, or stops sending data.

When the SDR stops working

RTL-SDR dongles occasionally lock up and stop sending data, it happens to everyone. When that happens, all of your streams lose audio, and the dashboard shows that the RTL-SDR requires attention. Try these steps in order:

  1. Reset the SDR. On the dashboard, select Configure RTL-SDR, then select Reset SDR. This restarts the dongle without unplugging it, and usually does the trick.
  2. Unplug it and plug it back in. If a reset doesn't help, and you're near the machine running NWR Stream Manager, do the classic "unplug it and plug it back in" solution that the IT people at your job always tell you. It's more effective than one might think.
  3. Restart the computer. If you aren't near the computer running NWR Stream Manager but can reach it remotely with a command line (shell access), here's another classic from the IT playbook: "turn it off and back on again", or in this case, restart the machine:
    sudo reboot
    NWR Stream Manager and your streams start again on their own once the computer is back up, if it was installed to run as a service.

If the problem keeps coming back, check the power supply to the computer and dongle, try a different USB port, and consider a powered USB hub. Weak power is a common cause of dongles locking up.

Setting up your first stream

A stream is one weather radio station, sent to one or more places called outputs. To add one, open Manage Streams and select Add stream. A step-by-step guide walks you through the entire process.

  1. Choose the station. Search by callsign, city, state or frequency, then select the station from the list of matches.
  2. Choose the output type: an Icecast mountpoint to stream online, or a sound card. See Output types.
  3. For Icecast, choose the streaming service: GWES Weather Radio, WeatherUSA, NOAA Weather Radio Org, or a custom Icecast server. Choosing a service fills in its settings for you. See Streaming services for how to get set up with each one.
  4. Choose the stream format, if the service lets you pick: MP3 works almost everywhere, while OGG can sound better while using less internet data, but isn't compatible with as many devices, though it should work on any modern computer or smartphone.
  5. Enter your Icecast details from the streaming service: usually the password and mountpoint, plus the server address and username for a custom server. NWR Stream Manager checks them by connecting to the server before it saves anything.
  6. Adjust audio quality, if offered. If you have no idea what these settings are, just select Finish. The defaults will work just fine in 90% of cases.

For a sound card, you choose the sound card, which of its left and right channels to use, and the volume instead. See Sound card outputs.

Once you finish, the stream starts right away. You can add more outputs to it later, so one station can go to several services and a sound card at once.

Output types: Icecast and sound cards

An output is a place a stream's audio is sent. There are two kinds:

Icecast mountpoint
Sends the station over the internet to an Icecast server, which is the kind of server weather radio streaming services use. This is how you get your station onto GWES Weather Radio, WeatherUSA, NOAA Weather Radio Org, or your own server. A "mountpoint" is simply the name of your stream's spot on the server, a bit like a web address.
Sound card
Plays the station through a sound card connected to the computer running NWR Stream Manager. This gives you a 24/7 audio feed, for example to feed an EAS ENDEC (yes, some hobbyists actually do this). See Sound card outputs.

Streaming services

There are three online services you can share your stream with. Each one works a little differently. When you choose one in NWR Stream Manager, its server settings are filled in for you and its requirements, such as the stream format, are applied automatically.

GWES Weather Radio

GWES Weather Radio is run by the Global Weather & EAS Society. Each streamer gets their own login details.

  1. Submit your stream to GWES. Go to weatherradio.org and select Submit a Stream, or use the link NWR Stream Manager shows when you choose GWES. Fill out the short form with details such as your name, your email address, the name GWES should use when crediting the stream, and the callsign of the station.
  2. Wait for your credentials. Within a few days, you should get an email from GWES with your Icecast credentials.
  3. Enter them in NWR Stream Manager. Add a stream (or an output), choose GWES Weather Radio, and enter the details from the email.

GWES requires streams to be in MP3 at a bitrate of 64 Kbps or higher. If you have no idea what that means, that's okay; NWR Stream Manager sets this for you.

WeatherUSA

  1. Create a WeatherUSA account at weatherusa.net before streaming.
  2. Add your stream. Once you've created your account and signed in, select My Services in the menu, then select Add my NOAA Weather Radio stream.
  3. Fill in the station information in the form and submit it. WeatherUSA then gives you your Icecast credentials.
  4. Enter them in NWR Stream Manager. Choose WeatherUSA when adding a stream. The server and username are filled in for you, so all you need to enter is your password and mountpoint, and choose MP3 or OGG. NWR Stream Manager suggests a mountpoint based on the callsign; make sure it matches the one WeatherUSA gave you.

Officially, WeatherUSA allows each person to stream one station. If you'd like to stream more than one, you can email WeatherUSA and ask to stream additional stations.

NOAA Weather Radio Org

NOAA Weather Radio Org uses a shared server, so there are no credentials to wait for.

  1. Set up the mountpoint. Add a stream (or an output) and choose NOAA Weather Radio Org. NWR Stream Manager does this for you: it fills in the server, the shared login, and the audio settings the site requires, and names the mountpoint for the station in the format the site uses (state, city and callsign).
  2. Submit your stream. Once the mountpoint is set up, a reminder appears in NWR Stream Manager with a link to the NOAA Weather Radio Org submission form. Your stream won't appear on the website until you fill it out and it gets approved.

If someone else is already streaming the same station to NOAA Weather Radio Org, they accept multiple feeds of the same station. In NWR Stream Manager, simply turn on Alternate stream and choose a number. This adds "-alt1", "-alt2" and so on to the mountpoint name. NOAA Weather Radio Org's Weather Radio Station Lookup Utility shows which mountpoints are already taken.

Your own Icecast server

Choose Custom Icecast server to stream anywhere else, such as a service not listed in NWR Stream Manager or an Icecast server you run yourself. If you select this option you have to do all the dirty work yourself and enter the server's address (host), port, username, password and mountpoint, and choose the format and audio quality.

Sound card outputs

A sound card output plays a station through a sound card connected to the computer running NWR Stream Manager. This isn't the device you're using to view this page. The main use is giving equipment a 24/7 feed of the station. For example, you might feed an Emergency Alert System ENDEC, which listens to weather radio for alerts to relay.

When adding a sound card output, you choose:

  • The sound card. Small, inexpensive USB sound cards will do, if you were wondering.
  • Channels: left, right, or both. Using one channel per station lets a single stereo sound card carry two stations.
  • Volume for that sound card only. It doesn't change the volume of your online streams.

I designed NWR Stream Manager to request exclusive access to sound cards, meaning it won't cooperate with PulseAudio or PipeWire, which ship with all the popular Linux desktop environments. If a sound card doesn't show up, or shows up but won't play, PipeWire is probably hogging it. Use a sound card that nothing else is using, or even better, run NWR Stream Manager on a computer without a desktop, such as a Raspberry Pi set up as a server. This behavior is intentional, as PipeWire and PulseAudio give applications a way to all use the same sound card to play their audio, which is the exact opposite of what you want when feeding EAS equipment.

If a USB sound card stops responding, open the stream's Outputs tab and choose Reset USB sound card from its More actions menu. Most sound cards aren't nearly as finicky as RTL-SDR dongles, but I have seen it on occasion where a sound card misbehaves, especially on PCs with weak USB power.

The streams table

Manage Streams lists every stream you've set up in a table, one stream per row:

Callsign
The station's callsign, such as WXN99.
Frequency
The station's frequency, one of the seven weather radio channels between 162.400 and 162.550 MHz.
Outputs
Number of outputs the stream is being sent to.
Signal
How well the station is being received, as a rating from Excellent to Poor. See Signal strength levels. This is the first thing to check if a stream sounds noisy.
Status
Enabled means the stream is running and all of its outputs are working. Needs attention means something is wrong: reception is bad, a failure occurred connecting to an Icecast mountpoint, or a sound card went belly up or was disconnected. The dashboard explains what the problem is. Disabled means the stream is stopped by the user; that one is self-explanatory.
Actions
The More actions menu lets you start or stop the stream, listen to it in your browser (Monitor), open its settings (Edit stream settings), or remove it.

Signal strength levels

NWR Stream Manager rates how clearly each station is received, from best to worst:

RatingWhat it means
ExcellentA strong, clear signal. The station sounds clean.
GoodA solid signal with little noise.
FairThe stream is usable, but has some background hiss.
PoorThis is the point where EAS decoding fails and listeners complain. When it gets bad enough to be unusable, the stream is marked as having bad reception.
No signalOnly static is being received. If it reaches this point, fallback audio will play if you have it enabled for the stream.

The rating is followed by a number in dB SNR (signal-to-noise ratio). Higher numbers are better. Generally this number should stay above 8 dB; anywhere below that number is the point where EAS equipment starts to fail and the stream sounds deplorable. If a station's signal is poor, the best improvements usually come from a better antenna, placing it higher up or outdoors, and checking the gain.

Stream settings

To change a stream's settings, open Manage Streams, open the stream's More actions menu and select Edit stream settings. Changes take effect right away.

Enabling, monitoring and signal

Above the tabs are three controls that show up no matter where you are in the settings page:

  • Enable this stream starts or stops the stream.
  • Monitor lets you listen to the stream in your browser. See Listening to a stream in your browser.
  • Signal shows the station's reception, just like the Signal column in the streams table, along with the signal and background noise levels in more detail.

Below that are tabs for each group of settings. Each is described in turn below.

Outputs

The Outputs tab lists every output the stream is sent to. Use the Show outputs selector to switch between Icecast outputs and Sound card outputs; you can only view one output type at a time.

Each output shows its status: Connected (or Enabled for sound cards) when it's working, Needs attention when it isn't, and Disabled when you've turned it off. Its More actions menu lets you disable or enable it, edit it, or remove it. For USB sound cards, the menu also has Reset USB sound card.

To add another output, select Add output, then choose Icecast mountpoint or Sound card. The steps are the same as when you set up your first stream:

  • Icecast: choose the streaming service, then enter its details. See Streaming services.
  • Sound card: choose the sound card, channels and volume. See Sound card outputs.

Every stream needs at least one output working. This means if the stream only has one output, you can't disable it. EAS recording counts as an output, so it is possible to have a stream that gets sent nowhere and only records alerts.

EAS recording

EAS recording saves every EAS alert the station broadcasts as an audio file on the computer running NWR Stream Manager. You can then play, download and manage the recordings in EAS alerts.

Enable EAS recording
Turns recording on for the station.
Pre-recording time
How many seconds of audio to keep from before the alert starts, from 0 to 10.
Post-recording time
How many seconds to keep recording after the alert ends, from 0 to 10.
Maximum recording time
The longest a single alert recording can be, in seconds.
Recording format
WAV for the best quality, or MP3 for smaller files.

If the computer runs low on storage, recording is paused automatically so the disk doesn't fill up completely.

Fallback audio

If a stream stops getting audio from the radio, or if there's no signal from the station, you can optionally enable fallback audio. When fallback audio is on, a voice announcement will play instead of listeners hearing static or dead air. The voice announcement is a prerecorded audio file of the VoiceText Paul voice, and it says the following: "This weather radio stream is currently experiencing technical difficulties. Please check back later."

Fallback delay
How long to wait after the station audio stops before fallback audio kicks in, from 30 to 120 seconds.
Seconds before restart
The fallback audio plays in a loop. This setting controls the pause before the audio repeats. Set it to 0 to repeat it without a gap.

As soon as radio audio comes back, the station plays again.

Audio effects

Audio effects shape how the stream sounds. Choose an effect from the list to see its settings.

NWR deemphasis

This is the key to a good sounding stream. Leave it on. Weather radio stations boost the high-pitched parts of their audio before transmitting, and every weather radio turns them back down when it plays the sound. This is called deemphasis. It makes the audio sound natural and keeps hiss down. Without it, the stream sounds harsh, tinny and hissy, very different from a real weather radio. Deemphasis is on for new streams.

Volume multiplier
Adjusts the stream volume. The default volume level is 1. A volume of 2 is double, a volume level of 0.5 is half.
Comfort noise
Mixes in a very quiet background hiss, like the faint noise some weather radios make during silence. Some listeners prefer it instead of hearing the transmitter background noise. The level can be set from -60 dB (quietest) to -30 dB (loudest).
Highpass
Removes low sounds, such as a 60 Hz hum, below the frequency you choose. Sharpness sets how gradual the cut is: 0 is gentle and natural sounding, and 10 cuts off very sharply. The frequency can't go above 900 Hz, so the 1050 Hz tone is never affected.
Lowpass
Removes high sounds above the frequency you choose, with the same sharpness setting as the highpass. It can't go below 2200 Hz, so SAME tones remain intact. This is separate from deemphasis.
Notch filter
Removes a narrow band of sound around one frequency, which is useful for getting rid of transmitter buzz or high-pitched whines that you'll encounter sooner or later. Choose the frequency to filter out and how wide the notch should be. A narrow notch removes just the whine; a wider one also removes the surrounding frequencies. To protect the alert tones, the notch can't be placed on them, and how wide it can be depends on how close it is to them.

Stream notifications

Once you've set up notifications, a Notifications tab appears in stream settings. Choose which problems with this stream should notify you:

  • Failed Icecast mountpoints: a streaming service can't be reached.
  • Disconnected sound cards.
  • Bad reception or no signal.
  • Recorded EAS alerts: a new alert has been saved (when EAS recording is on).

You'll also receive a notification when each of these problems clears up.

Test mode

Test mode swaps the real station for a simulated weather radio station, so you can check that your outputs and any connected alert equipment, such as an ENDEC, work properly without waiting for a real alert. The simulated station plays a looping test announcement, and it goes through your stream's outputs and audio effects just like the real station would.

Turn on Enable test mode on the Test Mode tab. When you turn it off, the real station audio returns.

Signal strength
Makes the simulated station stronger or weaker, from -80 to 0 dBFS. Lowering it lets you hear how your stream and equipment behave with a weak, noisy signal.
Send SAME test

Sends a complete test alert: the alert header tones, the long 1050 Hz alert tone, a spoken test message, and the end-of-message tones. Connected alert equipment should react to it just like a real alert.

Important: the test alert will be sent out to all enabled outputs, including streaming services, EAS recording and sound cards. It's sent as a practice/demo alert for a location code that doesn't exist (999000), which helps prevent anything from acting on it by mistake. Even so, disable any outputs you don't want it sent to first, especially public services such as GWES Weather Radio, NOAA Weather Radio Org or WeatherUSA. GWES Weather Radio, especially, is one you should disable, as they sometimes monitor users' streams for EAS alerts as part of their EAS Relay Network. Don't be an idiot with the SAME test feature.

Tone generators

While test mode is on, you can play tones along with the test announcement to simulate buzzes and whines, for example to try out the audio effects that remove them. There are four kinds: sine (a pure whine), square (a harsh, hollow buzz), triangle (a soft tone) and sawtooth (a bright, raspy buzz). Each can be turned on separately, with its own frequency (20 to 7000 Hz) and amplitude (loudness). Tones keep playing during a SAME test.

Checking the Mute tone generators checkbox silences all tone generators; unchecking it brings them back.

Test mode time limit

Test mode can do real harm if misused, since it can send alerts to equipment and to the public. To prevent abuse, there are limits on how long the test mode features can be used. Test mode can be used for up to 10 minutes in any hour. The time is shared by all streams: using test mode on one stream uses up time for all of them. Oh, and if you were getting any clever ideas, the limit does not reset if you restart NWR Stream Manager or reboot the computer.

The limit is "rolling": rather than resetting all at once on the hour, each minute of test mode you use comes back one hour after you used it. For example, if you used test mode from 2:00 to 2:03 and again from 2:30 to 2:37, you'd get 3 minutes back between 3:00 and 3:03, and the other 7 between 3:30 and 3:37. This means you can never use more than 10 minutes of test mode in any 60-minute stretch, no matter how you time it.

The Test Mode tab shows how much time you have left. When you've used it all, test mode turns off and the page tells you when more time will be available.

If time runs out, or you close the page, in the middle of a SAME test, the test is ended safely rather than cut off: the alert header tones and the 1050 Hz alert tone finish, then after 2 seconds of silence the end-of-message tones are sent, and the real station returns a second later. This makes sure connected equipment isn't left thinking an alert is still going on.

Only one person can use test mode at a time, and it turns off if you close the page. This means if you open NWR Stream Manager on your computer and enter test mode for a stream, then attempt to access test mode on your phone, you won't be able to.

Listening to a stream in your browser

You can listen to any running stream right in your browser, exactly as your listeners hear it, including your audio effects. Choose Monitor from the stream's More actions menu in Manage Streams, or turn on Monitor in its settings.

  • On Android, listening usually keeps going when you switch to another app or lock the screen.
  • On iPhone and iPad, listening stops when Safari goes into the background. This is a known limitation; there's nothing I can do about it, unfortunately.
  • If your internet connection is too unstable, listening stops and NWR Stream Manager tells you why.
  • In Firefox, Chrome and Edge, listening uses much less data and copes better with a slow connection when you open NWR Stream Manager with a secure https:// address. See Using a secure (https) address with Tailscale Serve.

EAS alerts

When EAS recording is on, every alert your streams record shows up under EAS alerts in the bar at the top. The link appears once the first alert has been recorded.

  • Choose a stream to see its alerts, newest first.
  • Select an alert to see its details: the type of event, the counties it covers, and when it was issued and when it expires. You can play the recording, download it, or remove it. Turn on Autoplay to play alerts one after another.
  • Export alerts downloads a group of recordings at once, and Delete alerts removes a group of them.

The dashboard also shows the most recently issued alerts.

Weather Radio Receiver

The Weather Radio Receiver is for when you just want to listen to NWR. After all, you have an RTL-SDR capable of capturing all 7 channels at once, so you should have a way to tune around the band. Open it from More, then Weather Radio Receiver.

  • Use Previous channel and Next channel to change frequency, and Play to start listening.
  • If an alert is broadcast while you're listening, a banner appears and shows what the alert is and the areas it covers. On phones, the banner only shows the alert type due to the small screen size unless you put the phone in landscape mode.

Listening in the background works the same way as monitoring a stream.

Notifications

NWR Stream Manager can send notifications to your phone or computer when something goes haywire: a stream goes down, a sound card stops working, reception gets bad, or the RTL-SDR gives up, and when said problems are resolved. You can also get notified when an alert gets recorded for streams with EAS recording enabled.

Notifications are sent through ntfy, a free notification service. You install the ntfy app and subscribe to a private "topic", which is like a private channel only you know the name of. NWR Stream Manager then sends its notifications to that topic.

Setting up notifications

  1. Open More, then Notification settings, and select Set up.
  2. Choose whether you have your own ntfy server. Most people should choose No, use ntfy.sh, the free public server, which doesn't need an account.
  3. Install the ntfy app. There are links for the Apple App Store and Google Play, or you can use the ntfy web app in your browser.
  4. Copy the topic secret that NWR Stream Manager shows. In the ntfy app, add a subscription and paste the topic secret as the topic name. You can subscribe on as many devices as you like.
  5. Choose the address your devices should open when you select a notification. Pick one your devices can reach. If you use Tailscale, choosing its address means notifications open NWR Stream Manager even when you're away from home.
  6. Select Send test notification to make sure it arrives, then select Finish.

Keep your topic secret private. Anyone who knows it can see your notifications and send their own. If you ever get notifications that didn't come from NWR Stream Manager, select Regenerate topic secret in Notification settings, and subscribe to the new topic in the ntfy app.

Choosing what you're notified about

After setup, choose what you want notifications for:

  • For each stream: open its settings and use the Notifications tab.
  • For the RTL-SDR: open Configure RTL-SDR and turn on Notify when the RTL-SDR requires attention.

Using an SDR from another NWR Stream Manager

Two computers running NWR Stream Manager can share an RTL-SDR with each other. One computer has the RTL-SDR plugged in, and the other uses it over the network as if it were plugged in there.

Why would I want this?

The best place for your RTL-SDR is right next to your antenna, often in an attic, on a roof or at the top of a mast, with a short cable. The shorter the cable between antenna and dongle, the less signal is lost. But that spot often isn't a good place to stream from. For example, if you monitor with an ENDEC, the ENDEC and its sound card probably live somewhere else in the house.

With sharing, a small computer such as a Raspberry Pi sits by the antenna with the RTL-SDR, and your main NWR Stream Manager elsewhere does the streaming, recording and sound card output using that RTL-SDR.

Pairing two NWR Stream Managers

Both computers need to be on the same network, or connected through Tailscale. You only have to pair them once, and after that either one can use the other's RTL-SDR.

  1. On the computer with the RTL-SDR, open Configure RTL-SDR. Under Sharing, turn on Share this RTL-SDR with paired devices, then select Pair a device. A 6-digit pairing code appears. It lasts for 5 minutes, so make sure you pair the two devices before it expires.
  2. On the other computer, open Configure RTL-SDR. Under Pair with another NWR Stream Manager, select Find devices, choose the other computer from the list, enter the pairing code, and select Pair. If it isn't in the list, you can enter its IP address instead if you know it.
  3. Still on the second computer, under SDR source, choose RTL-SDR shared by another NWR Stream Manager, then choose the paired computer under Shared SDR.

Paired computers are listed under Paired devices, where you can also remove them.

Network buffer

When using a shared RTL-SDR, network inconsistencies and dropouts have to be taken into account. When using an RTL-SDR connected directly over USB, there isn't much latency. However, the same can't be said for transmitting SDR data over the network. The Network buffer setting holds back a little radio data to ride out Wi-Fi hiccups and brief network dropouts. A bigger buffer is more reliable on a weak connection, but puts your audio a bit further behind real time. The delay applies to everything using the shared RTL-SDR, including monitoring and the Weather Radio Receiver. The buffer can be set between 0 and 10 seconds. You'll probably notice the delay the most when using the weather radio receiver, as depending on what the buffer is set to there will be a noticeable delay before the weather radio receiver plays audio or switches channels.

If a firewall runs on the computer with the RTL-SDR, you'll need to unblock TCP port 47432 and UDP port 47433.

Setting up Tailscale

NWR Stream Manager runs on a computer on your local network, meaning you can only access it when you're at home connected to your Wi-Fi. However, you probably want to be able to access NWR Stream Manager when you're away from home as well. Tailscale connects your devices together securely over the internet, as if they were all on the same home network. It has a free plan for personal use. With Tailscale you can:

  • Open NWR Stream Manager from your phone or laptop when you're away from home.
  • Have notifications open NWR Stream Manager wherever you are.
  • Share an RTL-SDR between two NWR Stream Managers in different places, such as a friend's house or a second site.

Tailscale needs to be installed on every device, including the devices with NWR Stream Manager installed and the computers, phones, and tablets you use to access NWR Stream Manager, and said devices all need to be on the same account and tailnet.

On the computer running NWR Stream Manager

  1. Open a command line (terminal) on the computer running NWR Stream Manager, and run the following command to install Tailscale:
    curl -fsSL https://tailscale.com/install.sh | sh
  2. Start Tailscale:
    sudo tailscale up
    After a few seconds, a web link will be printed to the terminal. Copy this link, open it in a browser and sign in to your Tailscale account (or create one) to add the machine.
  3. Find the computer's Tailscale address:
    tailscale ip -4
    It's a number starting with 100, such as 100.101.102.103.

On your phone, tablet or other computers

Install the Tailscale app from tailscale.com/download or from the Google Play Store or Apple App Store, and sign in with the same Tailscale account. For a second NWR Stream Manager, repeat the steps above on that computer.

Using it

With Tailscale on, you can open NWR Stream Manager from anywhere using its Tailscale address and port, for example http://100.101.102.103:8080. Once Tailscale is running on the computer with NWR Stream Manager, it also appears as a choice for where notifications should open, and paired devices can be found over Tailscale when sharing an RTL-SDR.

Using a secure (https) address with Tailscale Serve

What http and https mean

Every website you visit starts with either http:// ot https:// before the web address. Most addresses you visit start with https://. Both are ways for your browser to talk to a website, and the difference is the "s", which stands for secure:

  • http sends everything as is. Anyone who can see the traffic along the way can read it, including bad actors.
  • https scrambles (encrypts) everything between your browser and the website, so only the two of them can read it. It also proves the website is really the one you meant to visit. Most browsers show a padlock next to https addresses.

If you haven't noticed by now, when you open NWR Stream Manager, it starts with http://. That works, and on your home network or through Tailscale your connection is already protected in other ways. However, because browsers can't verify an http:// address, some of them, including Firefox, Chrome and Edge, turn off certain features there. The one that matters most is listening in your browser: on an http:// address, those browsers can't use the efficient audio format, so monitoring a stream or using the Weather Radio Receiver uses several times more data, and the audio can't adjust itself when your connection gets slow. Safari on iPhone and iPad isn't affected.

Giving NWR Stream Manager an https:// address fixes that. It needs a certificate, which is a kind of digital ID card proving the address is genuine. Getting one normally takes some technical work, but Tailscale can handle all of it for you. This is another reason I strongly recommend having Tailscale set up.

What Tailscale Serve does

If you use Tailscale, its Serve feature gives NWR Stream Manager a secure address that works on all of your Tailscale devices, such as https://my-pi.example-tailnet.ts.net, and takes care of the certificate for you. The annoying part is serve isn't turned on by default for new Tailscale accounts, so there are a few steps that need to be completed first. The good news is, once those steps are completed, you won't need to do them ever again.

Turn on secure addresses for your Tailscale account

  1. Sign in to the DNS page of the Tailscale admin console.
  2. MagicDNS should already be enabled unless you manually disabled it. If you manually disabled it, you'll need to enable it again. It gives each of your devices a web address, which the secure address is built from.
  3. Under HTTPS Certificates, select Enable HTTPS.

Your device names become public. Certificates are recorded in public logs that anyone can search, and those logs include the name of each device that gets a certificate, along with your Tailscale account's network name. Nothing else about your devices is shared, but if any of your device names contain something private, such as your full name or address, it might be a good idea to either get those devices off your tailnet or rename them.

Give NWR Stream Manager a secure address

Now that we've gotten the boring stuff out of the way, we can finally enable Tailscale Serve for NWR Stream Manager! Follow the steps below to turn it on:

p>
  1. On the computer running NWR Stream Manager, open a command line and run the following. If you started NWR Stream Manager on a different port than 8080, use that number instead.
    sudo tailscale serve --bg 8080
    The first time you do this, Tailscale may show a web link asking you to allow Serve on your account. Open the link, approve it, then run the command again.
  2. Tailscale shows the secure address NWR Stream Manager is now available at. You can see it again at any time with:
    tailscale serve status
  3. Open that https:// address on any device signed in to your Tailscale account. Only your own Tailscale devices can reach it; it isn't put on the public internet.

Serve keeps running in the background and comes back on its own after the computer restarts.

If you use notifications, NWR Stream Manager notices the secure address within a minute and lists it as Tailscale Serve under Open NWR Stream Manager from notifications in Notification settings. If that setting is on Automatic, notifications start opening the secure address on their own; you don't need to change anything.

To stop serving the secure address, run:

sudo tailscale serve reset

Other ways to get a secure address

You can also put NWR Stream Manager behind a reverse proxy with a certificate, such as Caddy or nginx, or reach it through a Cloudflare Tunnel. If you have no idea what any of that means, just follow the steps above. A reverse proxy or Cloudflare tunnel is more involved and is outside the scope of this help.

Accounts

Everyone who uses NWR Stream Manager signs in with an account. There are three types of accounts:

Owner
The account created during first-run setup. It can do everything, and it's the only account that can manage other accounts.
Administrator
Can set up and change the RTL-SDR, streams, outputs, EAS recording, audio effects and everything else, but can't manage accounts.
Read-only
Can look but not change anything: view streams, listen to them, use the Weather Radio Receiver, play and export EAS alerts, download I/Q recordings and view logs.

Creating accounts for other people

  1. Signed in as the owner, open More, then Manage accounts, and select Create account.
  2. Enter a username. Check Read-only account if the person should only be able to look and listen; otherwise they'll be an administrator.
  3. Select Create account. A temporary password appears. Copy it and give it to the person. It's only shown once, so be sure to send it to the person right away.

The first time they sign in, they'll be asked to choose their own password.

Passwords

  • To change your own password, open More, then Change account password. The owner does this from Manage accounts instead, by choosing Change account password from their own account's More actions menu.
  • If someone forgets their password, the owner can open Manage accounts and choose Reset password from that account's More actions menu. This gives them a new temporary password.
  • If the owner password is lost, NWR Stream Manager has to be stopped and its account database (the accounts.db file in its settings folder) deleted. The setup page then appears again so a new owner account can be created. Your streams and settings are kept. Starting to get the memo yet? Don't forget your password!

I/Q Recorder

For those of you that are more familiar with how RTL-SDRs work, the I/Q recorder may be of use to you. The I/Q Recorder saves the raw radio data the RTL-SDR receives, rather than audio. You won't need it for everyday streaming. It's mainly useful for advanced users, who may want to record a chunk of spectrum and analyze it in external SDR software such as SDR++ or SDR#. Open it from More, then I/Q Recorder.

  • New recording records either one stream's station or the entire weather radio band. You can have it stop on its own after a number of minutes.
  • While a recording is running, a banner at the top of every page reminds you.
  • Recordings can be downloaded in a few formats. Choose whichever one you need.

Warning: Recording and saving I/Q data will absolutely chew through disk space like nobody's business if you aren't careful. Keep an eye on the storage space shown while recording.

Logs

The Logs page, under More, shows a running record of what NWR Stream Manager is doing, along with whether the RTL-SDR is sending data. Most users won't need to touch or understand the logs, but if you report a bug, I may ask you to send me the log.

Storage

EAS alert recordings and I/Q recordings are saved on the computer running NWR Stream Manager, and they take up disk space over time. I/Q recordings especially can eat your storage for breakfast if you let them. The dashboard shows how much space is left and how much each kind of recording uses.

If the disk gets nearly full, NWR Stream Manager pauses EAS recording and stops any I/Q recording, so the computer doesn't run out of space completely. Delete old alerts in EAS alerts or old I/Q recordings to free up space; recording resumes once there's room again.

What is NWR Stream Manager?

NWR Stream Manager is an all-new solution to streaming your local NOAA Weather Radio stations to the internet, so anyone can listen to them online. It uses an inexpensive USB radio receiver called an RTL-SDR, plugged into a computer that stays on, such as a Raspberry Pi or a small home server.

One RTL-SDR hears the entire NOAA Weather Radio band at once. That means a single dongle can stream every weather radio station you can receive, all at the same time, without retuning anything.

You control everything from a web page that you open in any browser on your computer, phone or tablet. From there you can:

  • Stream stations to online services such as GWES Weather Radio, WeatherUSA and NOAA Weather Radio Org, or to your own Icecast server.
  • Play a station through a sound card, for example to feed Emergency Alert System (EAS) equipment.
  • Record and save EAS alerts as they are broadcast.
  • Listen to your streams, or tune around the weather radio band, right in your browser.
  • Get notifications on your phone when something goes belly up.
  • Share an RTL-SDR with another NWR Stream Manager on your network.

NWR Stream Manager runs on its own in the background. Once your streams are set up, they keep running without you. When it's installed to run as a service, it also starts again on its own whenever the computer restarts.

Why NWR Stream Manager was made

For a long time, most weather radio streams were run with a physical weather radio connected to a computer. That works, but it gets costly, since each station needs its own radio, and it isn't very efficient.

People who did stream with SDR dongles usually put together a chain of separate programs to do it: an SDR program such as SDR++ or SDR#, the Virtual Audio Cable program for Windows for virtual audio devices, and streaming software such as BUTT (Broadcast Using This Tool) to actually send the audio to the streaming service. That chain is more fragile than a stack of dominoes. All of these programs are desktop programs that can be closed just by your cat tripping over your keyboard, or by you elbowing the mouse wrong. Windows also isn't the most dependable choice for something that has to run unattended 24/7. And on top of all that, none of these programs apply deemphasis to weather radio audio, so the streams they made usually sounded shrill and tinny.

I created NWR Stream Manager to streamline and standardize streaming NOAA Weather Radio. Everything happens in one program that is built to run unattended, and it's all set up from one web page.

Why weather radio sounds different here: deemphasis

If you've listened to NOAA Weather Radio with other SDR software such as SDR++, SDR#, etc., you may have noticed that it sounded shrill, tinny or hissy, nothing like how your weather radios sound. That's because your weather radios are doing a little thing known as deemphasis.

FM is great, but it has one problem: raw FM sounds extremely unpleasant to listen to because its static is so shrill. So how did engineers get around this? Rather cleverly, actually. FM transmitters boost the high-pitched parts of their audio before sending the signal into the air. This is what's known as pre-emphasis. It helps speech stand out over the hiss. All FM transmitters do this, even the FM you might be accustomed to on the 88-108 MHz band for listening to music. Believe it or not, weather radio and two-way radios use FM as well, except the bandwidth is much narrower. I won't go into the science behind how FM transmitters work, but all you need to know is that the narrower the bandwidth is, the more static you'll hear as you get further away from the transmitter. Since NOAA Weather Radio and most walkie-talkies use a much narrower bandwidth than commercial FM stations, you'll sometimes hear a lot more static than you normally would. And like I said earlier, FM static is very unpleasant to listen to.

So you now know that FM transmitters boost the high parts of their audio, but why doesn't the audio sound shrill when you hear it on the radio? That's because FM receivers, including FM radios, walkie-talkies, and yes, even weather radios, do a little thing known as deemphasis. Deemphasis is basically the opposite of pre-emphasis; the radio lowers the volume of higher frequencies to undo the transmitter's pre-emphasis, getting rid of some of the shrill hiss and bringing the audio back to how it's supposed to sound.

Unbelievably, many SDR programs don't do deemphasis for narrowband FM. They raw dog the audio; as a result, the station sounds tinny and the static is almost painful to listen to. NWR Stream Manager always applies deemphasis to every stream, the same way a consumer weather radio does, so your streams sound like a real weather radio and it cannot be turned off.

What is an SDR?

SDR stands for software-defined radio. A regular radio has parts inside that are built to pick up one kind of signal, and you turn a dial to choose a station. A software-defined radio does most of that work in software instead. The hardware simply listens to a range of radio frequencies and hands everything it hears to the computer, and from there it's the computer's problem. That's both good and bad; I know.

The SDR used with NWR Stream Manager is called an RTL-SDR. It is a small USB stick, often called a dongle, a bit larger than a thumb drive, with a connector for an antenna. They are inexpensive and easy to find.

Unlike most conventional radios, which can only pick up one station at a time, an SDR can pick up multiple stations at a time if they're near enough in frequency. As such, NWR Stream Manager can receive all 7 NWR channels at the same time, allowing users to stream multiple stations with a single SDR dongle.

If you have only ever streamed with a physical weather radio, the main difference is this: instead of connecting a radio's headphone jack to a computer, you plug the RTL-SDR into the computer and connect your antenna to the RTL-SDR. NWR Stream Manager takes care of the rest.

What you need

  • An RTL-SDR dongle. One dongle can serve every station you stream.
  • An antenna that picks up the NOAA Weather Radio band, around 162 MHz. A good antenna placed high up, or outdoors, makes a bigger difference to reception than anything else.
  • A computer that stays on, running Linux, such as a Raspberry Pi or an old PC. This is the machine NWR Stream Manager runs on.
  • An internet connection, if you stream to online services.
  • A browser on any computer, phone or tablet on the same network, to set things up.

Installation instructions are in the NWR Stream Manager project page. Once it is installed and running, open its web address in your browser. When NWR Stream Manager starts, it shows the addresses it can be reached at, which usually end in :8080.

First run and creating your account

The first time you open NWR Stream Manager, you'll see a welcome page asking you to create an account. This account keeps other people on your network from messing with your streams.

  1. On the welcome page, select Next.
  2. Choose a username. It can use letters, numbers, dashes and underscores.
  3. Choose a password of at least 8 characters, and type it again to confirm it.
  4. Select Next, then Finish.

This first account is the owner account. It can do everything, including creating accounts for other people. See Accounts for more information on this.

Create your account right away. Until an account exists, anyone on your network who opens NWR Stream Manager sees the setup page. This means someone else in your house could create the owner account themselves, which wouldn't be good, would it? Open up your browser and complete the setup as soon as NWR Stream Manager is running for the first time.

Setup only works from a device on the same network as the computer NWR Stream Manager runs on, using that computer's network address, such as http://192.168.1.20:8080, or from a web browser on that computer itself (see What counts as the local network). Anywhere else, such as through a tunnel or proxy like Cloudflare Tunnel or Tailscale Funnel, you'll see a page asking you to finish setup from your own network instead. This keeps a stranger who finds your tunnel's address from creating the owner account. Once setup is done, you can sign in from elsewhere with two-step verification.

Signing in

After setup, your browser asks for your username and password whenever you open NWR Stream Manager. This is your browser's own sign-in box, not part of the page. Your browser remembers that you're signed in for a while, so you won't be nagged to sign in every time.

Configuring the RTL-SDR

Plug your RTL-SDR into the computer running NWR Stream Manager and connect your antenna to it. Then open Configure RTL-SDR from the bar at the top.

Pick your dongle from Active SDR. Dongles plugged into this machine are listed first, by serial number, followed by any RTL-SDRs that paired NWR Stream Managers share over the network. If yours isn't listed, check that it's plugged in and select Rescan.

Changes on this page take effect right away. You don't have to remember to click the save button. In an older browser, or with JavaScript turned off, make your changes, then select Save changes at the bottom of the page. Only what you changed is saved. Gain is chosen from a list, and Reset SDR asks you to confirm first.

Gain

Gain controls how strongly the RTL-SDR amplifies what it receives. Think of it like the volume on the antenna side, before any listening happens.

  • Keep gain between 30 and 35 dB for the best results in most places.
  • Gain that's too high overloads the dongle with other nearby signals, which causes interference and can make reception worse, not better.
  • Gain that's too low will weaken your signal and leave weak stations buried in static.

Leave Automatic gain control turned off. It tends to raise the gain too high, which, as mentioned above, can cause issues.

PPM correction

Some dongles, especially older ones, don't tune exactly to the frequency they're told to. They can end up a considerable amount off frequency, and if that isn't already bad enough, some dongles drift as they warm up. PPM correction compensates for this. You shouldn't have to play with it, but if you do, it's there. If you hear loud distortion when listening to your streams, that's probably frequency drift, and you can try tweaking the PPM until it sounds good as a troubleshooting step.

Bias tee

The bias tee sends a small amount of power up the antenna cable. It's only for equipment that needs that power to work, such as some antenna amplifiers (often called LNAs) or active antennas.

Most people will never need to turn this on. Only enable the bias tee if a piece of hardware connected to your antenna cable actually requires DC power. If you have hardware that needs it, you'll know, but for the vast majority of people, keep this setting switched off.

Alias filter strength

This controls how well NWR Stream Manager keeps signals from outside a station's channel from leaking in. Leave it at 100%. Lowering it saves some processing power on weak computers, at the cost of letting some interference through. If you slide this all the way down to 0, you'll probably hear a high-pitched whine if you're trying to listen to a weaker weather radio station and have a strong station nearby on a different frequency.

Notifications for the RTL-SDR

Once you've set up notifications, a Notify when the RTL-SDR requires attention option appears on this page. Turn it on to get a notification if the RTL-SDR disconnects, can't be opened, or stops sending data.

When the SDR stops working

RTL-SDR dongles occasionally lock up and stop sending data, it happens to everyone. When that happens, all of your streams lose audio, and the dashboard shows that the RTL-SDR requires attention. Try these steps in order:

  1. Reset the SDR. On the dashboard, select Configure RTL-SDR, then select Reset SDR. This restarts the dongle without unplugging it, and usually does the trick.
  2. Unplug it and plug it back in. If a reset doesn't help, and you're near the machine running NWR Stream Manager, do the classic "unplug it and plug it back in" solution that the IT people at your job always tell you. It's more effective than one might think.
  3. Restart the computer. If you aren't near the computer running NWR Stream Manager but can reach it remotely with a command line (shell access), here's another classic from the IT playbook: "turn it off and back on again", or in this case, restart the machine:
    sudo reboot
    NWR Stream Manager and your streams start again on their own once the computer is back up, if it was installed to run as a service.
  4. Ensure you have a stable internet connection. If you're using a remote machine's SDR, ensure you have a stable internet connection, otherwise you will experience audio dropouts. Use Ethernet for both machines when available, and if stuck using Wi-Fi, ensure both devices have a decent Wi-Fi signal.

Setting up your first stream

A stream is one weather radio station, sent to one or more places called outputs. To add one, open Manage Streams and select Add stream. A step-by-step guide walks you through the entire process.

  1. Choose the station. Search by callsign, city, state or frequency, then select the station from the list of matches.
  2. Choose the output type: an Icecast mountpoint to stream online, or a sound card. See Output types.
  3. For Icecast, choose the streaming service: GWES Weather Radio, WeatherUSA, NOAA Weather Radio Org, or a custom Icecast server. Choosing a service fills in its settings for you. See Streaming services for how to get set up with each one.
  4. Choose the stream format, if the service lets you pick: MP3 works almost everywhere, while OGG can sound better while using less internet data, but isn't compatible with as many devices, though it should work on any modern computer or smartphone.
  5. Enter your Icecast details from the streaming service: usually the password and mountpoint, plus the server address and username for a custom server. NWR Stream Manager checks them by connecting to the server before it saves anything.
  6. Adjust audio quality, if offered. If you have no idea what these settings are, just select Finish. The defaults will work just fine in 90% of cases.

For a sound card, you choose the sound card, which of its left and right channels to use, and the volume instead. See Sound card outputs.

Once you finish, the stream starts right away. You can add more outputs to it later, so one station can go to several services and a sound card at once.

In an older browser, each step is a page of its own. Type your search and select Search, choose the station from the matches, then select Next. For a sound card, the station starts playing through it as soon as you choose it, so you can check it's the right one; select Try these settings to hear a change to the channels or volume. Without JavaScript, this preview keeps playing for 5 minutes after your last change, or until you select Finish or Cancel.

Output types: Icecast and sound cards

An output is a place a stream's audio is sent. There are two kinds:

Icecast mountpoint
Sends the station over the internet to an Icecast server, which is the kind of server weather radio streaming services use. This is how you get your station onto GWES Weather Radio, WeatherUSA, NOAA Weather Radio Org, or your own server. A "mountpoint" is simply the name of your stream's spot on the server, a bit like a web address.
Sound card
Plays the station through a sound card connected to the computer running NWR Stream Manager. This gives you a 24/7 audio feed, for example to feed an EAS ENDEC (yes, some hobbyists actually do this). See Sound card outputs.

Streaming services

There are three online services you can share your stream with. Each one works a little differently. When you choose one in NWR Stream Manager, its server settings are filled in for you and its requirements, such as the stream format, are applied automatically.

GWES Weather Radio

GWES Weather Radio is run by the Global Weather & EAS Society. Each streamer gets their own login details.

  1. Submit your stream to GWES. Go to weatherradio.org and select Submit a Stream, or use the link NWR Stream Manager shows when you choose GWES. Fill out the short form with details such as your name, your email address, the name GWES should use when crediting the stream, and the callsign of the station.
  2. Wait for your credentials. Within a few days, you should get an email from GWES with your Icecast credentials.
  3. Enter them in NWR Stream Manager. Add a stream (or an output), choose GWES Weather Radio, and enter the details from the email.

GWES requires streams to be in MP3 at a bitrate of 64 Kbps or higher. If you have no idea what that means, that's okay; NWR Stream Manager sets this for you.

WeatherUSA

  1. Create a WeatherUSA account at weatherusa.net before streaming.
  2. Add your stream. Once you've created your account and signed in, select My Services in the menu, then select Add my NOAA Weather Radio stream.
  3. Fill in the station information in the form and submit it. WeatherUSA then gives you your Icecast credentials.
  4. Enter them in NWR Stream Manager. Choose WeatherUSA when adding a stream. The server and username are filled in for you, so all you need to enter is your password and mountpoint, and choose MP3 or OGG. NWR Stream Manager suggests a mountpoint based on the callsign; make sure it matches the one WeatherUSA gave you.

Officially, WeatherUSA allows each person to stream one station. If you'd like to stream more than one, you can email WeatherUSA and ask to stream additional stations.

NOAA Weather Radio Org

NOAA Weather Radio Org uses a shared server, so there are no credentials to wait for.

  1. Set up the mountpoint. Add a stream (or an output) and choose NOAA Weather Radio Org. NWR Stream Manager does this for you: it fills in the server, the shared login, and the audio settings the site requires, and names the mountpoint for the station in the format the site uses (state, city and callsign).
  2. Submit your stream. Once the mountpoint is set up, a reminder appears in NWR Stream Manager with a link to the NOAA Weather Radio Org submission form. Your stream won't appear on the website until you fill it out and it gets approved.

If someone else is already streaming the same station to NOAA Weather Radio Org, they accept multiple feeds of the same station. In NWR Stream Manager, simply turn on Alternate stream and choose a number. This adds "-alt1", "-alt2" and so on to the mountpoint name. NOAA Weather Radio Org's Weather Radio Station Lookup Utility shows which mountpoints are already taken.

Your own Icecast server

Choose Custom Icecast server to stream anywhere else, such as a service not listed in NWR Stream Manager or an Icecast server you run yourself. If you select this option you have to do all the dirty work yourself and enter the server's address (host), port, username, password and mountpoint, and choose the format and audio quality.

Sound card outputs

A sound card output plays a station through a sound card connected to the computer running NWR Stream Manager. This isn't the device you're using to view this page. The main use is giving equipment a 24/7 feed of the station. For example, you might feed an Emergency Alert System ENDEC, which listens to weather radio for alerts to relay.

When adding a sound card output, you choose:

  • The sound card. Small, inexpensive USB sound cards will do, if you were wondering.
  • Channels: left, right, or both. Using one channel per station lets a single stereo sound card carry two stations.
  • Volume for that sound card only. It doesn't change the volume of your online streams.

I designed NWR Stream Manager to take exclusive control of sound cards, meaning when you select a sound card, it grabs the device from ALSA directly. It won't play nice with PipeWire or PulseAudio, which are shipped with all the popular Linux desktop environments out there. If a sound card doesn't show up, or shows up but won't play, PipeWire is probably hogging it. Use a sound card that nothing else is using, or even better, run NWR Stream Manager on a computer without a desktop, such as a Raspberry Pi set up as a server. This behavior is intentional, as PipeWire and PulseAudio give applications a way to all use the same sound card to play their audio, which is the exact opposite of what you want when feeding EAS equipment.

If a USB sound card stops responding, open the stream's Outputs section and choose Reset USB sound card from its More actions menu. Most sound cards aren't nearly as finicky as RTL-SDR dongles, but I have seen it on occasion where a sound card misbehaves, especially on PCs with weak USB power.

The station's callsign, such as WXN99.
Frequency
The station's frequency, one of the seven weather radio channels between 162.400 and 162.550 MHz.
Outputs
Number of outputs the stream is being sent to.
Signal
How well the station is being received, as a rating from Excellent to Poor. See Signal strength levels. This is the first thing to check if a stream sounds noisy.
Status

Signal strength levels

NWR Stream Manager rates how clearly each station is received, from best to worst:

RatingWhat it means
ExcellentA strong, clear signal. The station sounds clean.
GoodA solid signal with little noise.
FairThe stream is usable, but has some background hiss.
PoorThis is the point where EAS decoding fails and listeners complain. When it gets bad enough to be unusable, the stream is marked as having bad reception.
No signalOnly static is being received. If it reaches this point, the stream's fallback action happens because you should not be streaming static.

The rating is followed by a number in dB SNR (signal-to-noise ratio). Higher numbers are better. Generally this number should stay above 8 dB; anywhere below that number is the point where EAS equipment starts to fail and the stream sounds deplorable. If a station's signal is poor, the best improvements usually come from a better antenna, placing it higher up or outdoors, getting it away from noisy electronics to the best of your ability, and checking the RTL-SDR gain.

Stream settings

To change a stream's settings, open Manage Streams, open the stream's More actions menu and select Edit stream settings. Changes take effect right away.

The settings page is laid out like Settings: a list of sections, with the section you choose beside it. On a phone, the list shows on its own; choose a section to open it, and select Back to stream settings to return to the list.

In an older browser, the sections are links that open one section at a time. Make your changes, then select Save changes. Be sure to save before moving to another section, or the changes you make will be lost. The Test mode section isn't there; see Test mode.

Enabling, monitoring and signal

Above the sections are three controls for the whole stream. On a phone they show with the list of sections.

  • Enable this stream starts or stops the stream.
  • Monitor lets you listen to the stream in your browser. See Listening to a stream in your browser.
  • Signal shows the station's reception, just like the Signal column in the streams table, along with the signal and background noise levels in more detail.

Below them is the list of sections, each described in turn below.

Outputs

The Outputs section lists every output the stream is sent to. Use the Show outputs selector to switch between Icecast outputs and Sound card outputs; you can only view one output type at a time.

Each output shows its status: Connected (or Enabled for sound cards) when it's working, Needs attention when it isn't, and Disabled when you've turned it off. Its More actions menu lets you disable or enable it, edit it, or remove it. For USB sound cards, the menu also has Reset USB sound card.

To add another output, select Add output, then choose Icecast mountpoint or Sound card. The steps are the same as when you set up your first stream:

  • Icecast: choose the streaming service, then enter its details. See Streaming services.
  • Sound card: choose the sound card, channels and volume. See Sound card outputs.

In an older browser, both output types are listed one after the other, and each output has an Edit output button. Its page is where you change it, and where you'll find Disable output, Reset USB sound card and Remove output; removing asks you to confirm first. To switch an Icecast output to another streaming service, choose the service and select Change service before filling in the rest. When adding a sound card output, select Refresh sound card list if yours isn't listed. Once it's added, select Try these settings to hear a change to the channels or volume before you select Finish.

Every stream needs at least one output working. This means if the stream only has one output, you can't disable it. EAS recording counts as an output, so it is possible to have a stream that gets sent nowhere and only records alerts.

EAS recording

EAS recording saves every EAS alert the station broadcasts as an audio file on the computer running NWR Stream Manager. You can then play, download and manage the recordings in EAS alerts.

Enable EAS recording
Turns recording on for the station.
Pre-recording time
How many seconds of audio to keep from before the alert starts, from 0 to 10.
Post-recording time
How many seconds to keep recording after the alert ends, from 0 to 10.
Maximum recording time
The longest a single alert recording can be, in seconds.
Recording format
WAV for the best quality, or MP3 for smaller files.

If the computer runs low on storage, recording is paused automatically so the disk doesn't fill up completely.

Stream archiving

Stream archiving keeps a continuous recording of the stream, exactly as listeners hear it, so you can go back and download any part of it later from Stream archives. Similar to EAS recording, it can be used on its own, without the stream having any additional outputs, meaning it's possible to have a stream that only archives audio without streaming anywhere.

Enable stream archiving
Turns archiving on for the stream. Once it's on, the same checkbox reads Disable stream archiving, which disables it. Disabling it keeps all archived audio.
Delete archived audio after
How long to keep archived audio: 24 hours, 1 week, 30 days, 180 days or 1 year. Anything older is deleted automatically. If choosing a shorter time would delete audio that's already archived, you're asked to confirm first; in an older browser, select Save changes a second time to confirm.

Archived audio will be slightly lower quality to preserve storage space. Audio is stored internally using Opus at 32 Kbps, using about 14 MB an hour per stream: roughly 350 MB a day, 2.4 GB a week, 10 GB for 30 days, 62 GB for 180 days or 126 GB for a full year.

Nothing is archived while the stream is stopped, while NWR Stream Manager isn't running, or while the RTL-SDR (or a shared RTL-SDR's network connection) isn't delivering audio; those times show as gaps. The only exception is if a stream has fallback audio enabled, in which case audio will still be archived when the SDR drops. If the computer runs low on storage, archiving is paused until there's room again.

Fallback action

The Fallback action section chooses what the stream does when it stops getting audio from the radio, or when there's no signal from the station.

Do nothing
Exactly what it sounds like; it does nothing. Users hear whatever garbage is being streamed, like static.
Disconnect from Icecast outputs
The stream disconnects from all of its Icecast servers, so it drops off the streaming services instead of sending dead air. The stream's Icecast outputs show as Disconnected (no station audio) when this happens. Sound card outputs keep playing.
Play fallback audio
The stream plays the fallback audio announcement in place of the station.
Fallback delay
For Disconnect from Icecast outputs and Play fallback audio, how long to wait after the station audio stops before the action happens, from 30 to 120 seconds.

As soon as the station is back, the stream switches back to the SDR audio, and disconnected Icecast outputs reconnect.

Audio effects

Audio effects shape how the stream sounds. They are all on one page, each under its own heading. Every stream also always has deemphasis, which isn't one of these effects and can't be turned off.

Volume multiplier
Adjusts the stream volume. The default volume level is 1. A volume of 2 is double, a volume level of 0.5 is half. The volume can be set from 0.1 to 2.
Comfort noise
Mixes in a very quiet background hiss, like the faint noise some weather radios make during silence. Some listeners prefer it instead of hearing the transmitter background noise. The level can be set from -60 dB (quietest) to -30 dB (loudest).
Highpass
Removes low sounds, such as a 60 Hz hum, below the frequency you choose. Sharpness sets how gradual the cut is: 0 is gentle and natural sounding, and 10 cuts off very sharply. The frequency can't go above 900 Hz, so the 1050 Hz tone is never affected.
Lowpass
Removes high sounds above the frequency you choose, with the same sharpness setting as the highpass. It can't go below 2200 Hz, so SAME tones remain intact.
Notch filter
Removes a narrow band of sound around one frequency, which is useful for getting rid of transmitter buzz or high-pitched whines that you'll encounter sooner or later. Choose the frequency to filter out and how wide the notch should be. A narrow notch removes just the whine; a wider one also removes the surrounding frequencies. To protect the alert tones, the notch can't be placed on them, and how wide it can be depends on how close it is to them.

Stream notifications

Once you've set up notifications, a Notifications section appears in stream settings. Choose which problems with this stream should notify you:

  • Failed Icecast mountpoints: a streaming service can't be reached.
  • Disconnected sound cards.
  • Bad reception or no signal.
  • Recorded EAS alerts: a new alert has been saved (when EAS recording is on).

You'll also receive a notification when each of these problems clears up.

Test mode

Test mode swaps the real station for a simulated weather radio station, so you can check that your outputs and any connected alert equipment, such as an ENDEC, work properly without waiting for a real alert. The simulated station plays a looping test announcement, and it goes through your stream's outputs and audio effects just like the real station would.

Turn on Enable test mode in the Test mode section. When you turn it off, the real station audio returns.

Test mode is only available in an up-to-date browser. While it's on, you need to see its countdown and be able to turn it off the moment something goes wrong, which an older browser can't promise, so the Test mode section doesn't appear there.

Signal strength
Makes the simulated station stronger or weaker, from -80 to 0 dBFS. Lowering it lets you hear how your stream and equipment behave with a weak, noisy signal.
Send SAME test

Sends a complete test alert: the alert header tones, the long 1050 Hz alert tone, a spoken test message, and the end-of-message tones. Connected alert equipment should react to it just like a real alert.

Important: the test alert will be sent out to all enabled outputs, including streaming services, EAS recording and sound cards. It's sent as a practice/demo alert for a location code that doesn't exist (999000), which helps prevent anything from acting on it by mistake. Even so, disable any outputs you don't want it sent to first, especially public services such as GWES Weather Radio, NOAA Weather Radio Org or WeatherUSA. GWES Weather Radio, especially, is one you should disable, as they sometimes monitor users' streams for EAS alerts as part of their EAS Relay Network. Don't be an idiot with the SAME test feature.

Tone generators

While test mode is on, you can play tones along with the test announcement to simulate buzzes and whines, for example to try out the audio effects that remove them. There are four kinds: sine (a pure whine), square (a harsh, hollow buzz), triangle (a soft tone) and sawtooth (a bright, raspy buzz). Each can be turned on separately, with its own frequency (20 to 7000 Hz) and amplitude (loudness). Tones keep playing during a SAME test.

Checking the Mute tone generators checkbox silences all tone generators; unchecking it brings them back.

Test mode time limit

Test mode can do real harm if misused, since it can send alerts to equipment and to the public. To prevent abuse, there are limits on how long the test mode features can be used. Test mode can be used for up to 10 minutes in any hour. The time is shared by all streams: using test mode on one stream uses up time for all of them. Oh, and if you were getting any clever ideas, the limit does not reset if you restart NWR Stream Manager or reboot the computer.

The limit is "rolling": rather than resetting all at once on the hour, each minute of test mode you use comes back one hour after you used it. For example, if you used test mode from 2:00 to 2:03 and again from 2:30 to 2:37, you'd get 3 minutes back between 3:00 and 3:03, and the other 7 between 3:30 and 3:37. This means you can never use more than 10 minutes of test mode in any 60-minute stretch, no matter how you time it.

The Test mode section shows how much time you have left. When you've used it all, test mode turns off and the page tells you when more time will be available.

If time runs out, or you close the page, in the middle of a SAME test, the test is ended safely rather than cut off: the alert header tones and the 1050 Hz alert tone finish, then after 2 seconds of silence the end-of-message tones are sent, and the real station returns a second later. This makes sure connected equipment isn't left thinking an alert is still going on.

Only one person can use test mode at a time, and it turns off if you close the page. This means if you open NWR Stream Manager on your computer and enter test mode for a stream, then attempt to access test mode on your phone, you won't be able to.

Listening to a stream in your browser

You can listen to any running stream right in your browser, exactly as your listeners hear it, including your audio effects. Choose Monitor from the stream's More actions menu in Manage Streams, or turn on Monitor in its settings.

  • On Android, listening usually keeps going when you switch to another app or lock the screen.
  • On iPhone and iPad, listening stops when Safari goes into the background. This is a known limitation; there's nothing I can do about it, unfortunately.
  • If your internet connection is too unstable, listening stops and NWR Stream Manager tells you why.
  • In Firefox, Chrome and Edge, listening uses much less data and copes better with a slow connection when you open NWR Stream Manager with a secure https:// address. See Using a secure (https) address with Tailscale Serve.

EAS alerts

When EAS recording is on, every alert your streams record shows up under EAS alerts in the bar at the top. The link appears once the first alert has been recorded.

  • Choose a stream to see its alerts, newest first.
  • Select an alert to see its details: the type of event, the counties it covers, and when it was issued and when it expires. You can play the recording, download it, or remove it. Turn on Autoplay to play alerts one after another.
  • Export alerts downloads a group of recordings at once, and Delete alerts removes a group of them.
  • In an older browser, alerts play with the browser's own player, and Previous alert and Next alert move between them. Autoplay works wherever the browser can run scripts. If the browser can't play the recording, select Download alert to listen to it. Removing or deleting alerts asks you to confirm on the page first.

The dashboard also shows the most recently issued alerts.

Stream archives

When stream archiving is on, Stream archives appears in the bar at the top once there's archived audio. Choose a stream to play its archive.

Playing an archive

The player starts an hour before the newest archived audio. Below Position, it shows the date and time of what's playing. Use the slider to move through the archive; with the keyboard, Left and Right Arrow move 10 seconds and Page Up and Page Down move 10 minutes. Back 10 seconds and Forward 10 seconds do the same from the buttons, the lock screen and headphone controls.

On a phone's lock screen, and wherever else your device shows what's playing, the archive's name includes the hour being played, such as Archive of WXN99, October 24, 2026, 1 AM EDT, and its length is that hour rather than the whole archive. The time elapsed is the minutes and seconds into that hour, as the clock would say, and dragging moves within it.

To go straight to a date and time, enter it under Jump to time and select Jump. If nothing was archived then, the page says so, and when the archive picks up again.

Gaps of up to a minute, times when nothing was archived, play as silence. Longer gaps are left out, so the player goes straight from before a gap to after it, and the time shown jumps ahead. When it reaches the newest archived audio, it stops and says you've caught up.

The archive keeps playing while you use other pages here. Playing the monitor, the Weather Radio Receiver or an EAS alert pauses it.

Deleting

To free up space, select Delete archived audio under the player, or on the download page where that's the page that opens. Choose to delete audio older than 24 hours, 1 week, 30 days, 180 days or 1 year, or all of it; only the choices that would delete something are listed, with how much each would delete. You're asked before anything is deleted. Audio is deleted an hour's file at a time, as when it's kept for a limited time (see stream archiving). Deleting all of it doesn't turn archiving off: it carries on from then. Read-only accounts can't delete archived audio.

Downloading

Select Download archived audio under the player. If your browser can't play the archive, the download page is what Stream archives opens to, with the stream to choose. Choose the date and time to start and end, up to 12 hours at a time, and a format:

Ogg Opus
The archived audio itself, copied without being re-encoded. The smallest and best quality.
MP3
64 Kbps, for players that don't support Opus. It's converted as it downloads, so a long one downloads more slowly than the others.
WAV
Uncompressed, and much larger: about 115 MB an hour.

Times are in the time zone of the computer running NWR Stream Manager, which the page names. Times can include seconds. Enter dates like 2026-10-23 and times like 17:40, 17:40:30 or 5:40 PM if your browser doesn't offer its own date and time pickers. The page lists the latest five gaps of a minute or more, times when nothing was archived, and says how many others there are. All gaps, however short, are silent in a download, so the audio stays in step with the clock.

Weather Radio Receiver

The Weather Radio Receiver is for when you just want to listen to NWR. Open it from More, then Weather Radio Receiver.

  • Use Previous channel and Next channel to change frequency, and Play to start listening.
  • If an alert is broadcast while you're listening, a banner appears and shows what the alert is and the areas it covers. On phones, the banner only shows the alert type due to the small screen size unless you put the phone in landscape mode.

I/Q Recorder

For those of you that are more familiar with how RTL-SDRs work, the I/Q recorder may be of use to you. The I/Q Recorder saves the raw radio data the RTL-SDR receives, rather than audio. You won't need it for everyday streaming. It's mainly useful for advanced users, who may want to record a chunk of spectrum and analyze it in external SDR software such as SDR++ or SDR#. Open it from More, then I/Q Recorder.

  • New recording records either one stream's station or the entire weather radio band. You can have it stop on its own after a number of minutes.
  • While a recording is running, a banner at the top of every page reminds you.
  • Recordings can be downloaded as raw I/Q data or as a WAV file, in float 32 bit, signed 16 bit or unsigned 8-bit samples. Choose whichever one your SDR software needs. A WAV file can't be larger than 4 GB, so for a long recording, only the WAV formats it fits in are offered: a 10 GB recording, for example, can still be downloaded as an unsigned 8-bit WAV file, but not as a float or 16 bit one. Unsigned 8-bit WAV is mainly for SDR Touch on Android, which only plays recordings at 256,000, 512,000 or 1,024,000 samples per second at the right pitch, so it's only offered for recordings at one of those rates. Signed 16 bit SDRangel saves the recording in SDRangel's own .sdriq format, which has no 4 GB limit and tells SDRangel the recording's frequency and start time. The SigMF formats save the recording as a .sigmf file, the standard SigMF archive, which also has no size limit. Some SDR software opens it as it is; for software that doesn't, such as GQRX, unpack it first (it's a tar file, which most archive tools can open) and open the .sigmf-data file inside.
  • In older browsers, the recording's timer keeps counting on its own wherever the browser can run scripts, and in most of them the file size and storage time remaining update too. With JavaScript turned off, refresh the page to see the latest. Removing a recording asks you to confirm on a page of its own.

Warning: Recording and saving I/Q data will absolutely chew through disk space like nobody's business if you aren't careful. Keep an eye on the storage space shown while recording.

Settings

Settings holds the settings that aren't about one stream or the RTL-SDR itself: General, your account or, for the owner, everyone's accounts, SDR sharing, notifications, fallback audio and, for the owner, Tailscale and Advanced. Open it from More, then Settings. Read-only accounts don't have Settings; they change their password from More, then Change account password.

General

General is the first setting in the list, and the one that opens when you open Settings. Its Instance name is the name of this NWR Stream Manager. It's shown at the top of every page, in your browser's title bar and tabs, and as the title of the notifications it sends, so if you run NWR Stream Manager on more than one computer, giving each one its own name makes it easy to tell them apart. Until you choose one, it's named after the computer it runs on, for example NWR Stream Manager on raspberrypi.

Location is optional. It's where this NWR Stream Manager or its RTL-SDR is, in your own words, for your reference, such as Attic SDR (Grand Rapids, MI). It's only stored on this computer. Once it's set, it's shown on the dashboard, under Configured SDR.

Below these are the version of NWR Stream Manager that's running and its uptime, how long it's been running since it last started, such as 2 days, 7 hours, 5 minutes.

The name and the location can each be up to 64 characters long. Once you've changed them, select Save changes; in an up-to-date browser, the button appears as soon as you change either one. Changes take effect right away, with no restart, and every open page picks them up. In an older browser, other open pages show it once they're refreshed. The owner and administrators can change the name.

Accounts

Everyone who uses NWR Stream Manager signs in with an account. There are three types of accounts:

Owner
The account created during first-run setup. It can do everything, and it's the only account that can manage other accounts.
Administrator
Can set up and change the RTL-SDR, streams, outputs, EAS recording, audio effects and everything else, but can't manage accounts.
Read-only
Can look but not change anything: view streams, listen to them, use the Weather Radio Receiver, play and export EAS alerts, download I/Q recordings and view logs.

Creating accounts for other people

  1. Signed in as the owner, open More, then Settings, then Accounts, and select Create account.
  2. Enter a username. Check Read-only account if the person should only be able to look and listen; otherwise they'll be an administrator.
  3. Select Create account. A temporary password appears. Copy it and give it to the person. It's only shown once, so be sure to send it to the person right away.

The first time they sign in, they'll be asked to choose their own password.

Passwords

  • To change your own password as an administrator, open More, then Settings, then Account. Read-only accounts open More, then Change account password. The owner does this from Accounts in Settings instead, by choosing Change account password from their own account's More actions menu.
  • If someone forgets their password, the owner can open Accounts in Settings and choose Reset password from that account's More actions menu. This gives them a new temporary password.
  • In an older browser, each account's actions are buttons instead of a More actions menu, and resetting a password or deleting an account asks you to confirm on the page first. A temporary password is shown on a page of its own; select Done once you've copied it.
  • If the owner password is lost, NWR Stream Manager has to be stopped and its account database (the accounts.db file in its settings folder) deleted. The setup page then appears again so a new owner account can be created. Your streams and settings are kept. Starting to get the memo yet? Don't forget your password!

SDR sharing

Two computers running NWR Stream Manager can share an RTL-SDR with each other. One computer has the RTL-SDR plugged in, and the other uses it over the network as if it were plugged in there.

Why would I want this?

The best place for your RTL-SDR isn't always the best place to stream from. For example, your SDR might be near your antenna installation or in a part of the house that gets better reception, while your EAS ENDEC and its sound card live somewhere else in the house such as your bedroom.

With sharing, a small computer such as a Raspberry Pi sits by the antenna with the RTL-SDR, and your main NWR Stream Manager elsewhere does the streaming, recording and sound card output using that RTL-SDR.

Pairing two NWR Stream Managers

To pair, both computers need to be on the same local network: pairing finds the other computer there, and only there, which also keeps strangers from pairing with yours. You only have to pair them once. After that, either one can use the other's RTL-SDR on the local network or, when both are on Tailscale, from anywhere. If a computer's Tailscale address changes, for example when you switch between Tailscale installed on the computer and NWR Stream Manager connecting to Tailscale by itself, the other one finds it again on its own: it's told the new address if the two are connected at the time, and otherwise looks for it among the devices on your tailnet.

  1. On the computer with the RTL-SDR, open More, then Settings, then SDR sharing. Under Sharing, turn on Share this RTL-SDR with paired devices, then select Pair a device. A screen with a 6-digit pairing code appears. Keep it open: the code lasts for 5 minutes, and pairing stops as soon as you leave this screen or close the tab. If you try to leave while pairing, NWR Stream Manager asks you to confirm first.
  2. On the other computer, open Settings, then SDR sharing. Under Pair with another NWR Stream Manager, select Find devices. A screen opens that keeps searching for NWR Stream Managers showing a pairing code, so the other computer appears as soon as it's ready. Choose it from the list, enter the pairing code, and select Pair. If it doesn't appear, you can choose Enter an address and type its IP address instead, if you know it.
  3. Still on the second computer, open Configure RTL-SDR, and choose Shared by the paired computer over the network from Active SDR. To go back to an RTL-SDR plugged into this computer, choose it from the same list.

Paired computers are listed in SDR sharing under Paired devices, where you can also remove them.

Note: if you're using an older browser, the page can't check in with NWR Stream Manager while it's open:

  • Turn sharing on or off, then select Save changes.
  • After Pair a device, pairing stays open until the code expires, even if you leave the page, or until you select Stop pairing. Refresh the page to see whether a device has paired. Depending on what browser you're using, the page may be able to notice by itself, but don't count on it.
  • Find devices searches once when the page opens. If the other computer isn't listed yet, select Search again.
  • Unpair asks you to confirm first.

A computer sharing its RTL-SDR can only be found by computers it hasn't paired with yet while it's showing a pairing code. The rest of the time, only the computers already paired with it can find it, so it stays hidden from everyone else on the network.

Network buffer

When using a shared RTL-SDR, network inconsistencies and dropouts have to be taken into account. When using an RTL-SDR connected directly over USB, there isn't much latency. However, the same can't be said for transmitting SDR data over the network. The Network buffer setting holds back a little radio data to ride out Wi-Fi hiccups and brief network dropouts. A bigger buffer is more reliable on a weak connection, but puts your audio a bit further behind real time. The delay applies to everything using the shared RTL-SDR, including monitoring and the Weather Radio Receiver. The buffer can be set between 0 and 10 seconds. You'll probably notice the delay the most when using the weather radio receiver, as depending on what the buffer is set to there will be a noticeable delay before the weather radio receiver plays audio or switches channels.

If a firewall runs on the computer with the RTL-SDR, you'll need to unblock TCP port 47432 and UDP port 47433.

Notifications

NWR Stream Manager can send notifications to your phone or computer when something goes haywire: a stream goes down, a sound card stops working, reception gets bad, or the RTL-SDR gives up, and when said problems are resolved. You can also get notified when an alert gets recorded for streams with EAS recording enabled.

Notifications are sent through ntfy, a free notification service. You install the ntfy app and subscribe to a private "topic", which is like a private channel only you know the name of. NWR Stream Manager then sends its notifications to that topic.

Setting up notifications

  1. Open More, then Settings, then Notifications, and select Set up.
  2. Choose whether you have your own ntfy server. Most people should choose No, use ntfy.sh, the free public server, which doesn't need an account.
  3. Install the ntfy app. There are links for the Apple App Store and Google Play, or you can use the ntfy web app in your browser.
  4. Copy the topic secret that NWR Stream Manager shows. In the ntfy app, add a subscription and paste the topic secret as the topic name. You can subscribe on as many devices as you like.
  5. Choose the address your devices should open when you select a notification. Pick one your devices can reach. If you use Tailscale, choosing its address means notifications open NWR Stream Manager even when you're away from home.
  6. Select Send test notification to make sure it arrives, then select Finish.

Keep your topic secret private. Anyone who knows it can see your notifications and send their own. If you ever get notifications that didn't come from NWR Stream Manager, select Regenerate topic secret in Settings, under Notifications, and subscribe to the new topic in the ntfy app.

Everything here works in an older browser too. Setup is a page per step, and Send test notification works on its last step as it does in an up-to-date browser. Afterwards, change the ntfy server or the address notifications open, then select Save changes; only what you changed is saved. Regenerate topic secret asks you to confirm first. Copy to clipboard appears wherever your browser can copy; otherwise select the topic secret and copy it yourself.

Choosing what you're notified about

After setup, choose what you want notifications for:

  • For each stream: open its settings and use its Notifications section.
  • For the RTL-SDR: open Configure RTL-SDR and turn on Notify when the RTL-SDR requires attention.

Fallback audio

Fallback audio is a voice announcement that streams can play instead of static or dead air when they have a problem. Whether a stream plays it is that stream's fallback action.

To set it up, open More, then Settings, then Fallback audio. This applies to every stream that plays fallback audio.

Announcement
Default plays the announcement that comes with NWR Stream Manager, a recording of the VoiceText Paul voice saying: "This weather radio stream is currently experiencing technical difficulties. Please check back later." Custom plays your own announcement, which you upload or record. Select Play announcement to listen to the one that's currently in use. If you switch back to Default, your custom announcement is kept, so you can switch to it again later.
Seconds before restart
The fallback audio plays in a loop. This setting controls the pause before the audio repeats. Set it to 0 to repeat it without a gap.

Custom announcements

Choose Custom to add your own announcement. A new announcement replaces the previous custom one and starts playing on streams right away, from the beginning.

  • Upload an announcement: select Upload announcement, then choose an MP3, OGG or WAV file that is one minute or shorter and under 10 MB. It's uploaded and checked as soon as you choose it.
  • Record an announcement: select Record an announcement to open the recording page. Your browser asks to use your microphone when the page opens, but nothing is recorded until you select Start recording. Speak into your microphone, then select the same button again, now called Stop recording. Recording stops by itself after one minute. Select Play recording to listen to it (the same button pauses it), then Save recording to use it. To try again, select Record again, which replaces the recording. Select Cancel to go back to Fallback audio without saving. Nothing is sent to NWR Stream Manager until you save it, and if you leave the page some other way with a recording you haven't saved, you're asked first. Headphones stop your screen reader or other sounds from being recorded too.

Every announcement is checked before it is used. Files that aren't MP3, OGG or WAV audio, that are damaged or incomplete, or that are silent are refused. Announcements that contain SAME tones are refused as well; sorry I spoiled your fun for those of you that wanted to trigger an EAN!

Browsers only allow microphone access on pages opened with a secure https:// address. To record an announcement, set up Tailscale Serve to give NWR Stream Manager a secure address. If you're accessing the page through plain old HTTP, then your only option is uploading a file. If you decline the browser's request to use your microphone, allow it in your browser's site settings and select Record again.

Advanced

Advanced chooses where NWR Stream Manager can be reached. Only the owner account has it. Unlike the rest of Settings, nothing here is saved until you select Save changes, and saved changes only take effect the next time NWR Stream Manager starts, for example when you restart it or the computer restarts. A wrong setting here can leave you unable to reach NWR Stream Manager at all, so double-check before you save. Advanced also says where NWR Stream Manager is listening right now.

Port
The number at the end of NWR Stream Manager's address, such as 8080 in http://192.168.1.10:8080. The default is 8080. Choose another one if something else on the computer already uses 8080. Ports below 1024 need extra permission, so pick one from 1024 to 65535. Once the new port takes effect, use it in the address you open, and in Tailscale Serve if you use it.
Only allow connections from this computer
NWR Stream Manager only accepts connections from the computer it's running on, so other devices on your network can't open it directly. This is for when something else on that computer passes connections along to it, such as Tailscale Serve or a reverse proxy like Caddy or nginx. Only turn it on once that's set up and working, or you'll have no way in from another device.

Command line options

When NWR Stream Manager is started with any of these options, they're used instead of the Advanced settings, and Advanced tells you so:

  • --port followed by a number listens on that port.
  • --localhost-only only accepts connections from this computer.
  • --bind-to-all-interfaces accepts connections from other devices on your network.
  • --host followed by an address listens on that one address.

If you installed NWR Stream Manager as a service with a service file from an older version, it may still start NWR Stream Manager with --host 0.0.0.0 --port 8080. Remove those from the service file's ExecStart line to use the Advanced settings.

If you've locked yourself out

If you run NWR Stream Manager yourself from a command line, start it with --bind-to-all-interfaces, and add --port 8080 if you don't remember the port you chose. Then fix the settings in Advanced and start it the usual way next time.

nwr-stream-manager --bind-to-all-interfaces --port 8080

If it runs as a service, delete the web-address.json file in its settings folder and restart the service. NWR Stream Manager goes back to port 8080, reachable from other devices, and your other settings are kept:

sudo rm /var/lib/nwr-stream-manager/web-address.json && sudo systemctl restart nwr-stream-manager

Restarting NWR Stream Manager

To restart NWR Stream Manager, open More, then Restart NWR Stream Manager, and confirm. The owner and administrators can do this; read-only accounts can't. NWR Stream Manager shuts down the same way it does when it exits, then starts again by itself. Streams stop for a few seconds and then start again, and an I/Q recording in progress stops. Everyone stays signed in. Every open page shows that it's restarting and reloads by itself once it's back; in an older browser, wait a few seconds, then select Done or refresh the page.

Restarting starts the version of NWR Stream Manager that's installed now, so it's also how to start using an update after installing it, without restarting the computer or the service. It also applies Advanced settings you've saved.

Setting up Tailscale

NWR Stream Manager runs on a computer on your local network, meaning you can only access it when you're at home connected to your Wi-Fi. However, you probably want to be able to access NWR Stream Manager when you're away from home as well. Tailscale connects your devices together securely over the internet, as if they were all on the same home network. It has a free plan for personal use. With Tailscale you can:

  • Open NWR Stream Manager from your phone or laptop when you're away from home.
  • Have notifications open NWR Stream Manager wherever you are.
  • Share an RTL-SDR between two NWR Stream Managers in different places, such as a friend's house or a second site.

Tailscale needs to be on the computers, phones and tablets you use to open NWR Stream Manager, all signed in to the same Tailscale account and tailnet. NWR Stream Manager itself can join your tailnet from Settings, as a device of its own, without installing Tailscale on the computer it runs on and without using the command line.

Connecting NWR Stream Manager from Settings

  1. Open More, then Settings, then Tailscale. Only the owner account has it.
  2. Select Connect to Tailscale. After a few seconds, a Sign in to Tailscale link appears.
  3. Open the link, sign in to your Tailscale account (or create one), and approve the device, which is named nwr-stream-manager.
  4. Settings then says it's connected and shows NWR Stream Manager's address on your tailnet, such as http://nwr-stream-manager.example-tailnet.ts.net/. Open that address from any of your devices on Tailscale.

Signing in to Tailscale needs a modern browser. The link takes you to Tailscale's own sign-in page, which older browsers most likely can't open. If yours can't, the Tailscale settings also show the link's address as text: open it on another device, such as your phone, and sign in there. In an older browser, refresh the Tailscale settings once you've signed in to see that it's connected.

A secure (https) address

Once it's connected, the Tailscale settings offer Use a secure (https) address, which gives NWR Stream Manager an https:// address on your tailnet, such as https://nwr-stream-manager.example-tailnet.ts.net/, with none of the Tailscale Serve steps. See what http and https mean for why it's worth having. Once the secure address works, the plain http:// one sends browsers to it, so old links keep working.

A secure address needs HTTPS turned on for your tailnet. New tailnets have it off; if yours does, the Tailscale settings show a Turn on HTTPS for your tailnet link. It opens Tailscale's Start using Serve page, which offers two things:

  • HTTPS certificates, already ticked: this is what NWR Stream Manager needs.
  • Tailscale Funnel (optional), also ticked to begin with. Funnel lets your tailnet's members make services on their devices reachable from the whole internet, not just your tailnet. NWR Stream Manager doesn't use it, so untick it unless you want it for something else.

Then select the button to turn them on. NWR Stream Manager notices by itself and sets up the secure address, which can take a minute the first time while its certificate is issued.

NWR Stream Manager's tailnet name becomes public. Certificates are recorded in public logs that anyone can search, and the log shows the name the certificate is for, such as nwr-stream-manager.example-tailnet.ts.net, which includes your tailnet's name. Nothing else about your tailnet is shared.

Reachable from the internet (Tailscale Funnel)

Once the secure address works, the Tailscale settings also offer Make NWR Stream Manager reachable from the internet (Tailscale Funnel), for opening it from devices that aren't on your tailnet, such as someone else's phone. Only the owner account has it, and you're asked to confirm before it's turned on. The public address is the secure address with :8443 after it, such as https://nwr-stream-manager.example-tailnet.ts.net:8443/; your own devices on Tailscale keep using the address without it.

Funnel needs your tailnet to allow it. If it doesn't, the Tailscale settings show an Allow Tailscale Funnel on your tailnet link to Tailscale's page for allowing it, and Funnel opens as soon as it's allowed.

Anyone on the internet can then open the sign-in page. Bots that watch the public certificate logs find new addresses like it within minutes and try passwords. Through Funnel, every account needs two-step verification as well as its password, and an account without it can't be used there at all. NWR Stream Manager also makes each wrong password wait longer, and the owner account can't be set up through Funnel. Still, give every account a long, unique password, and turn Funnel off when you don't need it.

Turning off the secure address, or signing out of Tailscale, turns Funnel off too.

NWR Stream Manager stays connected to your tailnet when it restarts. To take it off, select Sign out of Tailscale; to connect again, you'll sign in again. Signing out can't remove it from your tailnet's list of devices, so it stays in the Tailscale admin console, marked as expired, until you remove it there. Remove it before connecting again, or the new connection gets a numbered name, such as nwr-stream-manager-1. It can be reached on your tailnet even with Only allow connections from this computer turned on in Advanced, so you can keep it off your local network and still reach it through Tailscale.

Once it's connected, NWR Stream Manager also reaches the devices on your tailnet through it, even when the computer it runs on isn't on your tailnet: an Icecast server, or a paired shared RTL-SDR, named by its Tailscale name, such as my-pi, or its Tailscale address. Anything else, such as a local network address, is reached as usual. A computer paired while NWR Stream Manager was connected to Tailscale can use its shared RTL-SDR over Tailscale too, and the other way around.

If Tailscale is installed and connected on the computer itself, as described below, NWR Stream Manager uses that instead, and the Tailscale settings only say so: two connections to the same tailnet from one computer could get in each other's way. If NWR Stream Manager was connected to Tailscale by itself when it finds Tailscale installed, it signs itself out. Its own device then stays in the Tailscale admin console, marked as expired, until you remove it there.

Connecting from Settings needs Tailscale's library, libtailscale, which isn't part of every installation. If the Tailscale settings say it isn't installed, run nwr-stream-manager --build-libtailscale on the computer running NWR Stream Manager, with sudo in front if it runs as a systemd service. It needs git, a C compiler and Go 1.21 or later, downloads what it needs, including the newer Go that libtailscale asks for, and can take a few minutes. NWR Stream Manager uses the library as soon as it's built, without restarting, and installing or upgrading NWR Stream Manager again doesn't remove it. Or install Tailscale on the computer instead, as below.

Or: installing Tailscale on the computer running NWR Stream Manager

  1. Open a command line (terminal) on the computer running NWR Stream Manager, and run the following command to install Tailscale:
    curl -fsSL https://tailscale.com/install.sh | sh
  2. Start Tailscale:
    sudo tailscale up
    After a few seconds, a web link will be printed to the terminal. Copy this link, open it in a browser and sign in to your Tailscale account (or create one) to add the machine.
  3. Find the computer's Tailscale address:
    tailscale ip -4
    It's a number starting with 100, such as 100.101.102.103.

On your phone, tablet or other computers

Install the Tailscale app from tailscale.com/download or from the Google Play Store or Apple App Store, and sign in with the same Tailscale account. For a second NWR Stream Manager, repeat the steps above on that computer.

Using it

With Tailscale on, you can open NWR Stream Manager from anywhere. If you connected it from Settings, use the address shown there. If you installed Tailscale on the computer, use the computer's Tailscale address and port, for example http://100.101.102.103:8080. Once Tailscale is installed on the computer with NWR Stream Manager, it also appears as a choice for where notifications should open.

Using a secure (https) address with Tailscale Serve

What http and https mean

Every website you visit starts with either http:// or https:// before the web address. Most addresses you visit start with https://. Both are ways for your browser to talk to a website, and the difference is the "s", which stands for secure:

  • http sends everything as is. Anyone who can see the traffic along the way can read it, including bad actors.
  • https encrypts everything between your browser and the website, so only the two of them can read it. It also proves the website is really the one you meant to visit. Most browsers show a padlock next to https addresses.

If you haven't noticed by now, when you open NWR Stream Manager, it starts with http://. That works, and on your home network or through Tailscale your connection is already protected in other ways. However, because browsers can't verify an http:// address, some of them, including Firefox, Chrome and Edge, turn off certain features there. The one that matters most is listening in your browser: on an http:// address, those browsers can't use the efficient audio format, so monitoring a stream or using the Weather Radio Receiver uses several times more data, and the audio can't adjust itself when your connection gets slow. Safari on iPhone and iPad isn't affected. Browsers also only let a page use your microphone on an https:// address, so recording a custom fallback announcement needs one too.

Giving NWR Stream Manager an https:// address fixes that. It needs a certificate, which is a kind of digital ID card proving the address is genuine. Getting one normally takes some technical work, but Tailscale can handle all of it for you. This is another reason I strongly recommend having Tailscale set up.

What Tailscale Serve does

If you use Tailscale, its Serve feature gives NWR Stream Manager a secure address that works on all of your Tailscale devices, such as https://my-pi.example-tailnet.ts.net, and takes care of the certificate for you. The annoying part is serve isn't turned on by default for new Tailscale accounts, so there are a few steps that need to be completed first. The good news is, once those steps are completed, you won't need to do them ever again.

Turn on secure addresses for your Tailscale account

  1. Sign in to the DNS page of the Tailscale admin console.
  2. MagicDNS should already be enabled unless you manually disabled it. If you manually disabled it, you'll need to enable it again. It gives each of your devices a web address, which the secure address is built from.
  3. Under HTTPS Certificates, select Enable HTTPS.

Your device names become public. Certificates are recorded in public logs that anyone can search, and those logs include the name of each device that gets a certificate, along with your Tailscale account's network name. Nothing else about your devices is shared, but if any of your device names contain something private, such as your full name or address, it might be a good idea to either get those devices off your tailnet or rename them.

Give NWR Stream Manager a secure address

Now that we've gotten the boring stuff out of the way, we can finally enable Tailscale Serve for NWR Stream Manager! Follow the steps below to turn it on:

  1. On the computer running NWR Stream Manager, open a command line and run the command for how NWR Stream Manager runs there. Settings, under Tailscale, also shows the exact command for your computer.

    If it runs as a systemd service:

    sudo tailscale serve --bg unix:/var/lib/nwr-stream-manager/tailscale-serve.sock

    If you start it yourself:

    sudo tailscale serve --bg unix:$HOME/.local/state/nwr-stream-manager/tailscale-serve.sock
    The first time you do this, Tailscale may show a web link asking you to allow Serve on your account. Open the link, approve it, then run the command again.

    This passes requests to NWR Stream Manager through a socket that only Tailscale can use, rather than its port, so NWR Stream Manager knows for certain that a request came from someone on your tailnet: they don't need two-step verification. If you set up Serve with sudo tailscale serve --bg 8080 before, run sudo tailscale serve reset, then the command above. Serve still works with the port, but anything else on the computer could come in by the port the same way, so everyone through it is asked for a two-step verification code. Don't use --tcp or --tls-terminated-tcp with the socket: those pass on whatever a visitor sends, so anyone, even through Funnel, could claim to be on your tailnet. While Serve is set up that way, NWR Stream Manager refuses connections through it, and says so in its log.

  2. Tailscale shows the secure address NWR Stream Manager is now available at. You can see it again at any time with:
    tailscale serve status
  3. Open that https:// address on any device signed in to your Tailscale account. Only your own Tailscale devices can reach it; it isn't put on the public internet.

Serve keeps running in the background, and yes, it even survives reboots.

If you use notifications, NWR Stream Manager notices the secure address within a minute and lists it as Tailscale Serve under Open NWR Stream Manager from notifications in Settings, under Notifications. If that setting is on Automatic, notifications start opening the secure address on their own; you don't need to change anything.

To stop serving the secure address, run:

sudo tailscale serve reset

Other ways to get a secure address

You can also put NWR Stream Manager behind a reverse proxy with a certificate, such as Caddy or nginx, or reach it through a Cloudflare Tunnel. If you have no idea what any of that means, just follow the steps above. A reverse proxy or Cloudflare Tunnel is more involved and is outside the scope of this help. However, if you plan to use a Cloudflare Tunnel or reverse proxy, set up some sort of access policy, otherwise your instance will be accessible to the public internet. Do not rely on NWR Stream Manager's authentication alone to protect you from malicious actors. If you're using something like Cloudflare, please, do yourself a favor and configure Cloudflare Access for the tunnel.

Accessing NWR Stream Manager via the public internet

Most people probably access NWR Stream Manager via their local network or via their Tailnet. However, if you reach it from anywhere else, such as through a reverse proxy, a port forward, Cloudflare Tunnel or Tailscale Funnel, every account that reaches it this way must have two-step verification enabled. Most people fail to have any sort of protection infront of the doorway, so I've built some protection into NWR Stream Manager. You may think it's annoying, but I promise you'd be way more upset if a bad actor gained access to your streams than you woudl be turning on two-step verification.

Two-step verification

On your local network, your username and password are all you need to sign into your account. From anywhere else, such as a reverse proxy, a port forward, Cloudflare Tunnel or Tailscale Funnel, you need a 6-digit code from an authenticator app on your phone. Your local network is mostly secure. The public internet, however, isn't, and there are bad actors out there that may want to try and hack their way into your NWR Stream Manager instance if you're not careful. That's the reason why you must have two-step verification for each account that needs access outside your local network; simply entering a username and password in that instance doesn't cut it.

If you don't have two-step on for an account, that account can only be logged into if you're accessing NWR Stream Manager on your local network. If you try to log into that account through your reverse proxy or tunnel, you won't be allowed in.

What counts as the local network

NWR Stream Manager only skips the code when it's certain it isn't being accessed over the public internet. As such, these routes bypass the code:

  • A device on the same home or office network as the computer running NWR Stream Manager, connecting to it directly.
  • A web browser on the computer running NWR Stream Manager, at any of its addresses, localhost included. Due to Linux limitations, a browser on that computer only gets a free pass when it's running under the same user as NWR Stream Manager, whichever of the computer's addresses it uses. If you're running NWR Stream Manager via a systemd service, this most likely isn't the case. As a workaround, use another device on your local network.
  • A device on your tailnet, while NWR Stream Manager is on Tailscale itself (see Connecting NWR Stream Manager from Settings), or while Tailscale is installed on the computer it runs on and you use the computer's Tailscale address, or its secure address from Tailscale Serve (see Give NWR Stream Manager a secure address).

Everything else is sus and requires a code, including:

  • Any other program on the computer running NWR Stream Manager, such as a tunnel (ssh -R, cloudflared) or a reverse proxy, whichever of the computer's addresses it connects from, localhost included.
  • Anything through a tunnel or reverse proxy, even one on your own network. Tailscale Serve is the exception when it's set up as in Give NWR Stream Manager a secure address: then signed-in users on your tailnet don't need a code.
  • A port forwarded from the internet on your router, including when your router makes those connections look like they came from the router itself.
  • Anyone on a VPN the computer is connected to.
  • Every network but your tailnet, when the computer is directly on the internet, with a public IPv4 address of its own. On such a server, use Tailscale installed on it to reach NWR Stream Manager from your own devices, first-time setup included.
  • An address that looks like a tailnet's (100.64.0.0 to 100.127.255.255) but didn't come through Tailscale. Some internet providers give out the same addresses to their customers, so NWR Stream Manager checks that the connection really came from a device on your tailnet.

Setting up two-step verification

You must be accessing NWR Stream Manager via your local network or tailnet to set up two-step; you can't set it up any other way. If setting it up over the public internet were allowed, anyone who learned your password could set it up for themselves. If you for some reason can't access the local network, see Using a setup code. The owner should set it up at the end of first-time setup, while on the local network: no one can give the owner a setup code.

  1. Install an authenticator app on your phone if you don't have one, such as Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator or 1Password.
  2. In NWR Stream Manager, open More, then Two-step verification, and select Set up two-step verification. Every account has it, read-only accounts included.
  3. Add NWR Stream Manager to the app. Scan the QR code, open the setup link on your phone, or type in the key shown. If the app asks, it's a time-based key. (The QR code needs libqrencode, qrencode-libs on Fedora or libqrencode4 on Debian and Raspberry Pi OS; without it, use the link or the key.)
  4. NWR Stream Manager shows up in your authenticator app as NWR Stream Manager: <username> on <instance name>. If you type in the key, give it that name. Each computer you run NWR Stream Manager on has its own accounts, so set up two-step verification on each one, and the name tells you which code is for which.
  5. Enter the 6-digit code the app shows, and select Turn on two-step verification.
  6. You're then shown 10 recovery codes. Keep them somewhere safe, such as a password manager. They're only shown once; you won't be able to see them again.

Using a setup code

If you have users on your NWR Stream Manager instance that can't sign into NWR Stream Manager any other way except for the public internet, you can create a setup code for that user if you own the instance. A setup code works once and must be used within 24 hours of creation.

  • When you create an account, a setup code is shown along with its temporary password. If the person must access NWR Stream Manager via the public internet, you'll need to share the setup code with them in addition to the password.
  • For an existing account, you can open More, then Two-step verification, and select Create setup code beside it under Other accounts. This works from anywhere. A new setup code replaces the one before.

From outside the local network, after entering your password, enter the setup code where NWR Stream Manager asks for it. You'll then set up your authenticator app, as in the steps above, before you can do anything else. Once it's on, you're shown your recovery codes, and then go on to NWR Stream Manager, or to changing your temporary password if applicable.

Actually using two-step verification

From outside the local network, after your password, you'll be prompted for the code your authenticator app shows. If you don't want to do this every time you log in, tick Remember this device for 30 days. To make every remembered device ask again, select Forget remembered devices on the Two-step verification page. Note that resetting or changing your password forgets all of your devices as well.

Wrong codes are limited the same way as wrong passwords: after 5 attempts, you have to wait longer and longer, up to an hour for codes and 15 minutes for passwords. Oh, and if you didn't think that was bad enough, signing in doesn't start the count again, so someone guessing slowly can't get their free tries back whenever you sign in, and neither does restarting NWR Stream Manager. A day without a wrong try does.

What if I lose my phone?

I get it, it happens to the best of us; you drop your phone down the toilet, or maybe it falls out of your pocket while standing in a grocery store parking lot and a car runs it over. Either way, you no longer have access to that device. That's why recovery codes exist. You may enter one of your recovery codes in place of a code from your authenticator app. Each recovery code only works once. Then, from the local network, turn two-step verification off and set it up again on your new phone, or select Get new recovery codes if you're running low.

If someone has lost their phone and has no recovery codes left, you can create a setup code for them if you're the owner. After their password, they enter it in place of a code, and set up their new phone; the lost phone's codes stop working. From the local network, you can also turn two-step verification off for them instead, on your own Two-step verification page.

Note: If you are the owner and you lose your phone, however, your only way out is your recovery codes or local network access. Lose both of those, and you're out of luck.

Sign-in alerts

NWR Stream Manager keeps track of every incorrect attempt to access someone's account, as well as every incorrect two-step verification code for each account. If there are more than 3 incorrect attempts, a page saying so appears upon the next successful sign-in to the account in question. Select Continue to go on.

For each address the attempts came from, the page says:

  • How many wrong passwords and codes came from it, and when.
  • The IP address itself.
  • Roughly where the IP address is. If the IP is external, a location is given. Note that this location can be inaccurate, especially for phones on mobile data or attackers using VPN services. To find it, NWR Stream Manager sends the IP address, and nothing else, to ipinfo.io. Addresses on your local network or tailnet are never sent.
  • What kind of device it was, such as an Apple iPhone with Safari, a Windows computer with Chrome, or a program rather than a web browser. NWR Stream Manager logs whatever the device says it is, which attackers can change.

Note: A wrong two-step verification code means whoever tried to access your account successfully guessed your password. If this happens, change your password immediately.

The owner is also notified about more than 3 incorrect passwords for any other account, even if it isn't their own, as well as usernames that are invalid, such as admin, which bots often try. Everything is also written to the log, and the details are kept for 90 days.

Storage

EAS alert recordings and I/Q recordings are saved on the computer running NWR Stream Manager, and they take up disk space over time. I/Q recordings especially can eat your storage for breakfast if you let them. The dashboard shows how much space is left and how much each kind of recording uses.

If the disk gets nearly full, NWR Stream Manager pauses EAS recording and stops any I/Q recording, so the computer doesn't run out of space completely. Delete old alerts in EAS alerts or old I/Q recordings to free up space; recording resumes once there's room again.
